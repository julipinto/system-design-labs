<p class="ponte">Last lesson the excess got a limit and a signal. But every defense so far protects services, and a service can be restarted. The <strong>data</strong> cannot: what the SP primary lost, the replica does not have. What is left is to see how the data layer holds up the day the database goes down.</p>

<OneLiner>Data is the <strong>hardest</strong> layer to make resilient. The tools are <strong>copies</strong>, <strong>separating reads from writes</strong> and a <strong>cache that outlives the origin</strong>, each one trading a bit of consistency for availability.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Separate reads and writes.</strong> Reads go to the replicas (module 17), and losing a read replica is a trivial fallback: you send to another one.</li>
  <li><strong>The promotion.</strong> If the SP primary goes down, a replica becomes the primary. The <strong>RTO</strong> (the time until it is back) is <strong>35 s</strong>. With no primary, at 30 orders/s that is 30 × 35 = <strong>1,050</strong> write attempts with nowhere to go.</li>
  <li><strong>What is lost.</strong> The <strong>RPO</strong> measures the data the replica did not have yet: <strong>9</strong> orders if the promoted one is Rio's, <strong>24</strong> if it is Recife's, at 30/s (the asynchronous replicas from module 17 lag behind the primary).</li>
  <li><strong>The cache holds the origin.</strong> The catalog, with a 60 s TTL (module 5), an 80% hit rate and 640 req/s, sends only <strong>128 req/s</strong> to the database: 640 × 20%.</li>
  <li><strong>The cache outlives the origin.</strong> With <code>stale-if-error</code> (RFC 5861) the cache <strong>serves the old copy</strong> for a while when the origin fails. The CDN, with <code>max-age</code> (module 5), also holds when the origin is down.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">Resilience × consistency</h3>

<p>Serving the "old" price of 79.90 for 10 min is acceptable <strong>because the order command revalidates the current price</strong> (module 18). The read can be behind; the money cannot. A data <em>snapshot</em> for a fallback (lesson 9) follows the same rule: you decide in advance how much delay is tolerable, and where stale data is never accepted.</p>

<h3 class="sec">And if it goes wrong: the cache that goes cold along with the database</h3>

<p>The database goes down for 5 min. The catalog keys have a 60 s TTL: they expire and the hit rate collapses. When the database comes back, <strong>all the keys expired together</strong> and the <strong>640 req/s</strong> hit the origin at once, instead of the usual 128. That is the <strong>cache stampede</strong>: a spike that the freshly promoted database cannot take, and that knocks it down again. The fix has two parts: TTL with <strong>jitter</strong> (each key expires at a different moment) and <code>stale-while-revalidate</code> (serve the old copy while a single request fetches the new one).</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>A database does not restart.</strong> An RTO of 35 s and an RPO of 9 to 24 orders are the price the store accepts, and they need to be written down.</li>
  <li><strong>The cache is a shield.</strong> Of 640 req/s, 128 are left at the origin, and with <code>stale-if-error</code> it keeps serving even with the database down.</li>
  <li><strong>The same TTL on everything is a bomb.</strong> Keys that expire together come back together.</li>
</WhyItMatters>
