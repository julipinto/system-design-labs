<p class="ponte">Last lesson PSP-A spent 90 seconds at 8 s and the store had no defense. Now the team wants a number for the incident: "where did we end up?". The answer depends on <strong>what you count</strong>. The same incident gives 99.979% one way and 99.9965% the other.</p>

<OneLiner>Availability is measured <strong>by usage</strong> (errors ÷ attempts) or <strong>by time</strong> (<em>uptime</em>), and the two tell different stories. The number is only useful with a <strong>latency limit</strong> and an <strong>error budget</strong> next to it.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>By usage.</strong> Divide the errors by the attempts. The article gives 40 errors in 1,000 requests: <strong>4%</strong> error, <strong>96%</strong> availability. Whoever counts what an "error" is decides the number (see the end of the lesson).</li>
  <li><strong>By time.</strong> Subtract the <em>downtime</em> from the period. In 30 days there are <strong>43,200 min</strong>; with 180 min down, <strong>43,020</strong> are left, or <strong>99.58%</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>The nines.</strong> 99% leaves <strong>432 min</strong> per month down; 99.5%, 216; <strong>99.9%, 43.2 min (2,592 s)</strong>; 99.95%, 21.6; <strong>99.99%, 4.32 min (259 s)</strong>; 99.999%, <strong>25.9 s</strong>. Each extra nine divides the error time by ten.</li>
  <li><strong>Series and parallel.</strong> In series the math multiplies: 0.999³ = <strong>99.7003%</strong> (129.5 min) and 0.999⁴ = <strong>99.6006%</strong> (172.5 min). In parallel a failure only counts if <strong>all</strong> of them go down: two PSPs at 99.5% give 1 − 0.005² = <strong>99.9975%</strong> (1.08 min, against 216).</li>
  <li><strong>Error budget.</strong> A 99.9% usage-based SLO over the month's 12,960,000 attempts (5 orders/s) allows 0.1% failure: <strong>12,960</strong>. The 90 s incident burned 2,700: <strong>20.8%</strong> of the month.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">The same incident, two numbers</h3>

<p>At 20:20 there were 30 orders/s for 90 s: <strong>2,700 attempts</strong>, all failures. By usage, the month has 12,960,000 attempts and availability drops to 1 − 2,700 ÷ 12,960,000 = <strong>99.979%</strong>. By time, it was 90 s or 1.5 min out of 43,200, and the number is <strong>99.9965%</strong>. The time-based number looks better because it counts the clock and forgets that Black Friday has far more people per second than the middle of the night. Both are right; they answer different questions.</p>

<p>Without protection the damage grows: the exhausted pool takes the whole checkout down and the retry storm keeps <code>pagamentos</code> out for longer. In an example with <strong>5 minutes</strong>, 30 × 300 = 9,000 failures, <strong>69.4%</strong> of the month's budget burned by a single incident.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Latency is availability too</h3>

<p>A response that arrives in 8 s with status <code>200</code> is a success for the server and a failure for the client: they already closed the app. That is why the SLI (the indicator) needs a limit, like "answered within 1 s". Without it, the store can be at 100% success by status while nobody can pay. The <strong>SLO</strong> is the target on that indicator (99.9% good attempts in the month) and the error budget is what is left of it.</p>

<h3 class="sec">And if it goes wrong: three ways to measure badly</h3>

<p>The first is <strong>counting only <code>5xx</code></strong>. The slow PSP produces client <em>timeouts</em> that never become a status on the server, and the number looks great. The second is the <strong>average that lies</strong>: 99.9% over the month can hide an hour at 0% on Black Friday, because usage is concentrated and the month is long. The third is using <strong>series and parallel with dependent failures</strong>: the 99.9975% math only holds if the two PSPs fail independently. Two PSPs in the same cloud, with the same credential, do not.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>"How long it stayed up" has two meanings.</strong> By usage it counts orders; by time it counts the clock. Say which one you mean when you quote the number.</li>
  <li><strong>Series multiplies and parallel protects.</strong> Four hops at 99.9% end at 99.6006%; PSP-B in parallel recovers more than 200 min per month, as long as it fails independently.</li>
  <li><strong>The error budget becomes a decision.</strong> A 90 s incident spends 20.8%; an unprotected 5 min one spends 69.4%. You can say when to stop shipping and fix things.</li>
</WhyItMatters>
