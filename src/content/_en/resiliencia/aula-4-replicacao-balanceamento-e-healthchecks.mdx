<p class="ponte">Last lesson the radius of a piece became how much of the store depends on it. The cheapest defense is to <strong>not have just one piece</strong>. The store already has 10 replicas of <code>catalogo</code> behind <code>lb-loja</code> and 2 of <code>pagamentos</code> (modules 7 and 14). What is left is to see what the balancer does when <strong>one of them hangs</strong>.</p>

<OneLiner><strong>Several replicas behind a balancer that knows who is alive</strong> is the cheapest defense. What says who is alive is the <strong>healthcheck</strong>, and a badly written healthcheck takes down the store it was supposed to protect.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>The health URL.</strong> The application exposes an address, and the balancer asks it at intervals. The replica that answers stays in the pool; the one that fails stops receiving traffic.</li>
  <li><strong>The time until it leaves.</strong> Module 7 sets an interval of <strong>5 s</strong> and <strong>3 failures</strong> in a row: the hung replica takes <strong>15 s</strong> to leave.</li>
  <li><strong>What is lost in those 15 s.</strong> One of the catalog's 10 replicas hangs at 640 req/s: it was receiving 64 req/s and, in 15 s, loses <strong>960</strong> requests. One of the 2 <code>pagamentos</code> replicas hangs at 30/s: it was receiving 15/s and loses <strong>225</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>Outlier detection.</strong> Envoy also removes from rotation whatever errors in a row, without waiting for the healthcheck interval. The two mechanisms add up.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">Shallow and deep</h3>

<p>The <strong>shallow</strong> healthcheck checks "the process responds": it opens the port and returns <code>200</code>. The <strong>deep</strong> one goes further and checks dependencies, for example it runs a query on the database or pings the PSP. The deep one looks more honest and is where the danger lives.</p>

<h3 class="sec">And if it goes wrong: the deep healthcheck that queries the PSP</h3>

<p>PSP-A starts answering in 8 s. The <code>pagamentos</code> healthcheck queries the PSP, times out and says "sick". <strong>Both</strong> replicas get sick at the same time, because the problem is not in either of them. The balancer removes <strong>all</strong> of them: the store has no <code>pagamentos</code> even though the replicas were perfect.</p>

<p>Envoy has a safeguard against this, the <strong><em>panic threshold</em></strong>: below <strong>50%</strong> healthy replicas (the default) it <strong>ignores health</strong> and balances across all of them. But counting on panic is a matter of luck. Ejecting the innocent does not help and can cause the blackout.</p>

<p>The rule is simple: the healthcheck that <strong>removes from the pool</strong> checks only what <strong>the replica controls</strong> (memory, queue, live process). A bad dependency is handled with a <em>circuit breaker</em> (lesson 8) and a <em>fallback</em> (lesson 9), not by ejecting replicas.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>A replica is the cheap defense.</strong> Two copies behind the balancer turn "service down" into "half the capacity".</li>
  <li><strong>15 s is a long time.</strong> 960 requests on the catalog and 225 on <code>pagamentos</code> go through a dead replica before it leaves the pool.</li>
  <li><strong>The deep one ejects the innocent.</strong> A slow dependency is not a disease of the replica.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<p class="gancho">Copies and a balancer that carefully removes them from the pool solve the hung piece. But the same call can reach them twice, through a retry, a network failure or an impatient app. What is left is what happens when the same call is repeated.</p>
