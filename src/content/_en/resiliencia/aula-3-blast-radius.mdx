<p class="ponte">Last lesson the 90 s incident burned 20.8% of the error budget. But the number does not say <strong>what stopped</strong>: the whole store or just a part? That question sets the size of the damage before it happens, and its name is <strong>blast radius</strong>.</p>

<OneLiner>The <strong>blast radius</strong> is how much of the store <strong>stops</strong> when one component fails. Designing is making it <strong>small</strong> and <strong>known</strong>: you can say, before the incident, what disappears.</OneLiner>

<DoIt>
  <p>In the figure, in the <strong>Blast</strong> scenario, take down <code>shard-2</code> and then <code>recomendacoes</code>. Compare the stain: one wipes out a section of the screen, the other wipes out <strong>60% of the rows</strong> in the database, and notice that <code>shard-2</code> is the one that holds <code>mkt-01</code>.</p>
</DoIt>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Ask "what if it stops?".</strong> The article suggests four questions for each component: what stops working, what works in part, how long until I'm back and whether it creates inconsistency.</li>
  <li><strong>Answer with the store.</strong> The table below applies the four to the components that already exist: each row has a different radius.</li>
  <li><strong>Divide by N.</strong> With N equal compartments, each one takes 1/N: <strong>33.3%</strong> for N = 3, <strong>10%</strong> for 10, <strong>1%</strong> for 100 and <strong>0.1%</strong> for 1,000.</li>
  <li><strong>Check the load.</strong> The math assumes they all weigh the same. With <code>mkt-01</code> at 40% of the rows (module 16), <code>shard-2</code> takes <strong>0.4 + 0.6 ÷ 3 = 60%</strong> of the rows and the other two take <strong>20%</strong> each.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<p>Two answers deserve more attention. <code>catalogo</code> looks isolated, but the same service serves <code>bff-app</code> and <code>bff-web</code> (module 9): a hidden dependency whose failure wipes out both channels at once. And the SP primary row is the only one that creates <strong>inconsistency</strong>, the fourth question.</p>

<h3 class="sec">And if it goes wrong: the math that assumes equal load</h3>

<p>Three <em>shards</em> "give 33%". They do, if the customers weighed the same. With <code>mkt-01</code> concentrating 40% of the rows, taking down <code>shard-2</code> wipes out 60% of them, for the same 1/3 of the customers. One <em>shard</em> down affects <strong>1/3 of the customers</strong>, but <strong>20% or 60% of the rows</strong>, depending on which one. The article assumes uniform load and the 1/N math is only the best case.</p>

<p>The second way to get it wrong is the <strong>shared radius</strong>. Two dependencies that use the same connection pool are in the same radius, even if they look separate in the diagram. Lesson 13 shows this with <code>pagamentos</code>.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>The radius is a design decision.</strong> You can list, before the incident, what each failure wipes out.</li>
  <li><strong>1/N is the best case.</strong> With <code>mkt-01</code> at 40%, the radius of <code>shard-2</code> goes from 33.3% to 60% of the rows.</li>
  <li><strong>A hidden dependency adds radii.</strong> The shared <code>catalogo</code> and the single connection pool put everything inside the same circle.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={3}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">What each extra compartment costs</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>More compartments, smaller radius, more pieces.</strong> From 3 to 100 <em>shards</em> the radius drops from 33.3% to 1%, but you have to route, migrate and operate 100 things. The break-even point depends on what the radius costs per minute.</li>
    <li><strong>Cells.</strong> Isolating the store by region, each with its own full stack, is the limit of this idea (module 26).</li>
  </ul>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Correction to the source article: 1/N only with uniform load</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>"The more compartments, the smaller the radius".</strong> This holds when the load is uniform. The article does not discuss uneven load, and that is what decides which <em>shard</em> is dangerous.</li>
  </ul>

<p class="gancho">With the radius drawn, the simplest defense is to have more than one copy of what can fall. What is left is how the replicas know who is alive, and what happens when that warning is wrong.</p>
