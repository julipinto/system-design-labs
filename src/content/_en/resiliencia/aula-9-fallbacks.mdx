<p class="ponte">Last lesson the PSP-A breaker opened and <code>pagamentos</code> started answering right away that it would not call. But Lena still wants to pay. The breaker only <strong>decides to stop</strong>; what offers another path is the <em>fallback</em>.</p>

<OneLiner>The <strong><em>fallback</em></strong> is the alternative path to the <strong>same goal</strong>, and it always sacrifices something: cost, accuracy or consistency. A fallback nobody uses has a hidden defect.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Reactive: PSP-B.</strong> When PSP-A fails, <code>pagamentos</code> sends the charge to PSP-B (p50 of 300 ms, against 180 ms for A). That was the design in module 12.</li>
  <li><strong>Reserve value.</strong> The carrier's shipping quote falls back to the <strong>table shipping</strong> when it fails: Recife <strong>15.00</strong>, SP <strong>9.00</strong>, Rio <strong>12.00</strong>. Caio's 1058 pays Rio's 12.00.</li>
  <li><strong>Snapshot and queue.</strong> A <em>snapshot</em> (the credit limit from the last known value) answers when the service is down. An <strong>asynchronous flow</strong> sends the email to a queue and delivers it later.</li>
  <li><strong>Contractual.</strong> The second quote partner costs more: A at <strong>R$ 0.03</strong> and B at <strong>R$ 0.10</strong> per query (the article's numbers). In 10 min at 30/s that is 18,000 queries: <strong>R$ 540</strong> against <strong>R$ 1,800</strong>, an extra cost of <strong>R$ 1,260</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>Proactive.</strong> It sends <strong>1%</strong> of the traffic to the fallback all the time: 30 × 1% = <strong>0.3/s = 18 per minute</strong>. That way you find out before the incident whether it works.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">Thread 3: <code>"recomendado": []</code></h3>

<p>In module 9 <code>bff-app</code> answered without recommendations in 100 ms. That was a fallback: the goal (showing the page) was met with less content. Now the <code>pagamentos</code> fallback does the same for the payment, except the sacrifice here is money (PSP-B is slower) rather than content.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Why the proactive one is worth it</h3>

<p>AWS warns that a <strong>rarely exercised</strong> fallback has a latent defect: the code that only runs on failure is the least tested. The company prefers "continuously exercised code paths". Sending 1% of the traffic to PSP-B costs 18 calls per minute, and the price buys the certainty that the credential has not expired.</p>

<h3 class="sec">And if it goes wrong: three fallbacks that make the failure worse</h3>

<p>The first is the <strong>broken fallback nobody tested</strong>: PSP-B with an expired credential is only discovered at 20:20, with 100% of the traffic on top of it. The second is the <strong>fallback that takes down the fallback</strong>: all the traffic falls on PSP-B, which has less capacity, and PSP-B goes down too. Limit the fallback with its own breaker and a queue. The third is the <strong>inconsistent fallback</strong>: the credit limit <em>snapshot</em> approves beyond the real limit. That is a business decision, and it needs to be on record.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Every fallback sacrifices something.</strong> PSP-B is 120 ms slower, partner B costs R$ 1,260 more in 10 min and the table shipping may not be the real price.</li>
  <li><strong>A fallback that does not run rots.</strong> The proactive 1% is the continuous test.</li>
  <li><strong>The fallback needs a limit too.</strong> Otherwise the primary's failure becomes the second one's failure.</li>
</WhyItMatters>
