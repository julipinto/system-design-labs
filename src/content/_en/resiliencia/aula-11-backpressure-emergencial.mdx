<p class="ponte">Last lesson the store switched off recommendations to hold the entrance. But the excess that already got through is still inside the queues. The <code>meus-pedidos</code> projector from module 18 receives <strong>120 events per second</strong> and processes <strong>100</strong>. What is left is making the consumer <strong>tell whoever sends</strong> that it needs to slow down.</p>

<OneLiner><strong><em>Backpressure</em></strong> is the consumer telling the producer to slow down. The "emergency" kind is when the signal comes from outside the normal rhythm: a <strong>full queue</strong> or a <strong>high delay</strong>. It needs a <strong>bounded</strong> queue and a <strong>signal</strong>.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>The deficit.</strong> 120/s arrive and 100/s leave: <strong>20/s</strong> are left over and stay in the queue.</li>
  <li><strong>Bounded queue.</strong> With a limit of 5,000, it fills in 5,000 ÷ 20 = <strong>250 s</strong> (4 min 10 s). Once full, it has to refuse: the producer finds out.</li>
  <li><strong>Unbounded queue.</strong> In 10 min that is 12,000 events in the queue and <strong>120 s</strong> of delay (12,000 ÷ 100), and the delay keeps growing.</li>
  <li><strong>Draining.</strong> When the crisis passes and arrivals drop to 20/s, the queue of 12,000 only drains at 100 − 20 = 80/s: <strong>150 s</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>The signals.</strong> Bounded queue, <code>429</code> with <code>Retry-After</code> (RFC 6585 and RFC 9110), consumer pause, <code>request(n)</code> in reactive <em>streams</em>, the TCP window. They all say the same thing: "slower".</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">Dropping by priority</h3>

<p>Under overload, it is better to refuse <code>SHEDDABLE</code> traffic (marketing, reports) early than to delay everything. That is <em>load shedding</em>: dropping. The paid order needs the projector; the email campaign can wait or be dropped. The order is the same as in lesson 10.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Three things the article mixes up</h3>

<p><strong><em>Rate limiting</em></strong> limits the rate per client at the door (token bucket, module 8). <strong><em>Throttling</em></strong> slows down whoever went over their quota, instead of refusing. <strong><em>Load shedding</em></strong> refuses the excess from <strong>everyone</strong> when the store is overloaded. All three are the entrance to the defense, and each answers a different question: how much each one may send, how much to slow down and what to drop right now.</p>

<p>The article also ties backpressure to the error budget (reduce ingestion when the budget runs out) and to service meshes, which can apply the signals in the proxy without touching the code (module 10).</p>

<h3 class="sec">And if it goes wrong: "queue it in memory"</h3>

<p>The article treats "queuing the processing in memory" as a form of backpressure. Without a limit, it only <strong>hides</strong> the overload: latency grows with nobody seeing it, memory fills up and the process dies <strong>with the queue inside</strong>, taking the 12,000 events with it. Backpressure needs a <strong>bounded</strong> queue and a <strong>signal</strong> to the producer. Without the signal, the queue only postpones the problem.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Without a limit, there is no signal.</strong> The unbounded queue delays 120 s in 10 min and hides the problem.</li>
  <li><strong>The 429 hands the decision back to whoever sends.</strong> A producer with <code>Retry-After</code> knows when to come back.</li>
  <li><strong>Dropping is better than delaying everything.</strong> With the queue at its limit, SHEDDABLE goes first.</li>
</WhyItMatters>
