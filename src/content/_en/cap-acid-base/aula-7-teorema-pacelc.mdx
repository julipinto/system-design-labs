<p>The last lesson left a gap: CAP says nothing about latency, and the network is healthy almost all the time. PACELC fills that gap, and Lena's sale #101 shows the price.</p>

<OneLiner>PACELC completes CAP: if there is a Partition, choose between Availability and Consistency; else (Else), choose between Latency and Consistency, and the "else" is what happens almost all the time.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>CAP only looks at the failure. With the network working there is also a price: <strong>distance costs milliseconds</strong>. There are two ways to pay it.</li>
  <li><strong>Consistent write (EC):</strong> first, SP only answers "ok" after Rio and Recife confirm. Lena waits <strong>~80 ms</strong>, and with a slow network (300 ms between the cities) the wait becomes ~300 ms.</li>
  <li><strong>Fast write (EL):</strong> the other option is for SP to write and answer in <strong>~5 ms</strong>, sending the copy afterward. In exchange, if her brother checks Recife right after, he still sees <code>estoque 5</code> (v1) instead of 4: a stale read, for about 80 ms.</li>
  <li><strong>Putting the two halves together:</strong> "PA" or "PC" says what the system does in a partition; "EL" or "EC" says what it does without one. Hence PA/EL, PC/EL, PA/EC and PC/EC.</li>
  <li><strong>In the store,</strong> each operation picks its own: cart in PA/EL (fast and always up), balance and transfer in PC/EC (correct, even if slower).</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li>It answers the interview question: "and when the network is healthy, what do you give up?". Almost always the answer is <strong>latency vs. consistency</strong>.</li>
  <li>It also explains why "strong" global databases are slow to write and "eventual" databases are fast.</li>
  <li>Daniel Abadi proposed PACELC in 2010 precisely because CAP doesn't have the L for latency.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <p>These are classifications from the source article and from Abadi. Several depend on configuration, like MongoDB's <code>w</code>: treat them as a starting point.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Details of each combination</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>PA/EL:</strong> DynamoDB and Cassandra. Accepts divergence to get availability and speed.</li>
    <li><strong>PC/EL:</strong> Abadi cites Yahoo's PNUTS: asynchronous replication day to day, but it refuses writes in a partition.</li>
    <li><strong>PA/EC:</strong> unusual. Keeps strong consistency when normal and, on failure, accepts divergence (often with CRDTs).</li>
    <li><strong>PC/EC:</strong> traditional SQL databases, etcd, Spanner. The price is response time.</li>
  </ul>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Quick summary: CAP, PACELC, ACID and BASE</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>CAP:</strong> in a partition, C or A.</li>
    <li><strong>PACELC:</strong> in a partition, A or C. Without a partition, L or C.</li>
    <li><strong>ACID:</strong> transaction guarantees (A, C, I, D). Goes with higher consistency and latency.</li>
    <li><strong>BASE:</strong> availability, soft state and eventual consistency. Goes with low latency.</li>
  </ul>

<p>With the network cut or healthy, the question is the same: does this operation prefer to wait or to take a risk? Whoever designs a replicated system answers that operation by operation, before the failure.</p>
