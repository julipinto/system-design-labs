<p>The last lesson ended with a warning: the CP and AP labels are approximations. The modern critiques go further and say that "pick 2 of 3" hides more than it shows. They look at the same store, with the databases in SP, Rio and Recife.</p>

<OneLiner>"Pick 2 of 3" oversimplifies: partitions are rare, consistency and availability are degrees and not switches, and the choice should be made per operation, not for the whole database.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Partitions are rare.</strong> Most of the time the store has its three databases talking, like in sale #101. Without a partition, you can have consistency and availability together: the theorem only forbids it <em>during</em> the failure.</li>
  <li><strong>And even during the failure, C and A are degrees.</strong> Between "always the newest data" and "any old data" there are middle grounds: read from the leader, read with a quorum, accept a delay of up to 200 ms.</li>
  <li><strong>With degrees, the choice becomes per operation.</strong> Inside the same store: checking out the plush cat purchase (refuse in a partition), adding to the cart (always accept), showing the storefront (can be stale).</li>
  <li><strong>A partition has three phases (Brewer, 2012):</strong> detect (the 3,000 ms timeout expired), enter partition mode (limit what is risky) and recover (merge the versions and fix errors).</li>
  <li><strong>Recovery is the part people forget, and it's design too.</strong> If #102 and #103 fought over the last unit, the store would cancel one of the orders and give an R$ 20 coupon. The card charge is postponed until the network is back.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<div class="callout why" role="note">
  <h3>Kleppmann's critique (2015) in 3 lines</h3>
  <p>It attacks the definitions you saw in lesson 4.</p>
  <ul>
    <li>CAP's definitions are narrow: C is linearizability, A is "every request to a live node gets an answer", and only network failures count.</li>
    <li>Many databases don't fit in any box: it depends on how they're configured and which operation you look at.</li>
    <li>CAP says nothing about latency: a system that answers in two minutes is still "available" by the definition.</li>
  </ul>
</div>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li>Instead of asking "is our database CP or AP?", ask "what happens to <em>this operation</em> in a partition, and how do we fix it afterward?".</li>
  <li>A good design decision comes with its recovery plan: detect, limit, merge and compensate.</li>
  <li>And the latency critique, which CAP leaves out, is the doorway to PACELC (next lesson).</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={3}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Brewer, twelve years later</h4>
  <p>In 2012, Brewer himself wrote that CAP forbids only a small part of the possible design space. He argues that subsystems, operations, data and even users can have different choices, and that the focus should be the <strong>invariants</strong>: which rules you protect in a partition and which you can break and fix later.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Invariants in the store</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Can break and fix:</strong> the cat's stock going to -1 (selling more than there was). You cancel an order.</li>
    <li><strong>Always protect:</strong> Lena's balance doesn't go negative (<code>CHECK saldo &gt;= 0</code>): an R$ 200 request with R$ 150 in the account is refused.</li>
    <li><strong>Postpone:</strong> the card charge, until the network is back.</li>
  </ul>
  <h4 class="deep-h">The CP and AP labels</h4>
  <p>Kleppmann suggests dropping the labels and reasoning with precise definitions per operation. The source article has a table of "flavors" with labels per database, and the text itself shows a case where Cassandra appears as CP in one section and AP in another: a good example of why the label alone misleads.</p>

<p>Almost all of this is about the moment of failure, but the network is healthy nearly all the time. What's left is to see what a system gives up during all that time, when there is no partition at all. That's the question PACELC asks.</p>
