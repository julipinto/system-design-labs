<p class="ponte">The last lesson made it clear: there is no single transaction, no lock that can hold out for the Pix, and no <em>two-phase commit</em> that talks to the PSP. What's left is to <strong>do one step at a time and know how to undo each one</strong>. That is the Saga's proposal: each service does its own <strong>local transaction</strong>, and each step has a matching <strong>compensation</strong>.</p>

<OneLiner>Break the operation into <strong>local transactions</strong> T1…Tn, each with its own <strong>compensation</strong> C1…Cn. Either all of them pass, or the ones that already passed are compensated <strong>in reverse order</strong>. The compensation is not a <code>ROLLBACK</code>: it is a <strong>new transaction</strong> that undoes the <strong>business effect</strong>. And the Saga has no isolation: the in-between states show up for others.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Steps and compensations.</strong> Each local step has an undo: creating the order (compensation: <code>CancelarPedido</code>), charging (<code>EstornarCobranca</code>), reserving stock (<code>LiberarEstoque</code>) and scheduling delivery (<code>CancelarEntrega</code>).</li>
  <li><strong>Reverse order.</strong> If step 3 fails, the saga compensates 2 and then 1. In 1057, the stock failed at the reserve step: the charge is undone and the order is canceled, in the reverse order of what was done.</li>
  <li><strong>The compensation is a new transaction.</strong> The refund is <strong>one more entry</strong>, not an erasure: <code>c-7732</code> stays on the statement and the refund shows up next to it. The PSP may charge a fee that doesn't come back, and the "order confirmed" email that already went out can't be recalled: you send another one.</li>
  <li><strong>Three kinds of step.</strong> The <strong>compensable</strong> one has an undo (charge, reserve). The <strong>pivot</strong> is the point of no return: in the store, the truck leaving. The <strong>retryable</strong> one comes after the pivot and is only repeated until it works (notify the customer).</li>
  <li><strong>Order matters.</strong> What is <strong>hard to undo goes last</strong>. Charging hurts more than reserving: that's why, in lesson 10, the store reserves <strong>before</strong> charging.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: the dirty read</h3>

<p>The Saga is <strong>ACD without the I</strong>: it has atomicity (by compensation), consistency and durability, but <strong>no isolation</strong>. While 1057's saga is in <code>RESERVANDO</code>, the T-shirt stays reserved. If the saga is going to fail further on, for a few seconds the stock shows "sold out" to <strong>other customers</strong> who could never have bought that unit and who, by the time the reservation is released, have already left. Nobody did anything wrong: the in-between state is visible.</p>

<p>Three countermeasures (microservices.io):</p>

<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Semantic lock.</strong> The <code>RESERVA_PENDENTE</code> state is visible: whoever reads knows that unit may come back.</li>
  <li><strong>Commutative updates.</strong> Operations that give the same result in any order (add and subtract), so one saga doesn't get in the way of another.</li>
  <li><strong>Reread the value.</strong> Before changing, check that the data is still what the saga read.</li>
</ol>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Local step plus compensation.</strong> That's the trade: you give up the atomicity of one transaction for pieces that can be undone.</li>
  <li><strong>Compensating is not going back in time.</strong> The business effect is undone by another transaction, and may not give everything back (the PSP fee).</li>
  <li><strong>Order decides the cost.</strong> Reserving before charging swaps a refund for a stuck reservation (lesson 10).</li>
  <li><strong>Without isolation, the middle shows.</strong> Whoever reads during the saga sees states that may be undone.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <p>The split into compensable, pivot and retryable comes from Richardson (<em>Microservices Patterns</em>, a book; no verified link). The base article doesn't have it.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the base article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Atomicity and rollback.</strong> The Saga has atomicity <strong>by compensation, not by rollback</strong>, and <strong>no isolation</strong> (microservices.io: "lack of isolation"). There is also no automatic rollback: the developer writes each compensation. The countermeasures above don't appear in the article (there are also "by value" updates, the pessimistic view and a version file).</li>
    <li><strong>Compensation is not an exact undo.</strong> It can be another transaction (a refund, a new email), it may <strong>not give everything back</strong> (Microsoft: canceling a reservation "might not entitle the customer to a complete refund"), it has to be <strong>idempotent and repeatable</strong> and sometimes it fails (then it's a person; lesson 7). The article treats compensation as always possible.</li>
    <li><strong>Order of the steps.</strong> The article doesn't say the step that's hard to undo goes last.</li>
  </ul>

<p class="gancho">The saga is defined: local steps and compensations in reverse order. What's left is <strong>who decides</strong> the next step and when to compensate: each service, looking at what the previous one published, or a single one that knows the whole map?</p>
