<p class="ponte">Module 11 ended with the <code>estoque</code> reserving the last cat without selling it twice: replica lock, distributed lock, a pool of 10 connections. Bia took order 1049 and Caio got a <code>409</code>. But reserving the cat is only half of the sale. The <code>pedidos</code> still has to tell the <code>pagamentos</code> "charge 88.90 for order 1049", and that sentence travels over a connection, in a format, with a number of bytes. Now the cat is gone, and Davi shows up, from São Paulo, who wants the blue mug and pays with Pix at a PSP, a third-party payment provider. The question of this module: when one service calls another and waits for the answer, in what format, over which protocol, and how does the other side call back?</p>

<OneLiner>A synchronous call means the caller stops and waits for the answer before moving on: it stays simple and the error comes back right away, but the speed and availability of the caller now depend on everyone behind it.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>At <strong>14:05:00.000</strong> Davi's app creates order <strong>1050</strong>: blue mug (product 7) at <strong>19.90</strong> plus shipping to SP at <strong>9.00</strong>, <strong>28.90</strong> in total, or <strong>2890</strong> cents, paid with Pix. The charge at the PSP will be called <code>c-7731</code>.</li>
  <li>The order crosses a chain: Davi's app → <code>gw-loja</code> → <code>pedidos</code> → <code>pagamentos</code> → PSP. At each hop, the caller sits idle until the answer comes back. That is a <strong>synchronous</strong> call.</li>
  <li>The timings in this example, on the server: <code>gw-loja</code> → <code>pedidos</code> 5 ms, the stock reservation from module 11 20 ms, <code>pedidos</code> → <code>pagamentos</code> 5 ms and <code>pagamentos</code> → PSP <strong>180 ms</strong>, because the PSP is on the internet. The sum is 5 + 20 + 5 + 180 = <strong>210 ms</strong>, and Davi only sees an answer after the last hop.</li>
  <li>Waiting has one advantage: the order is clear and the error comes back along the same path. If the <code>estoque</code> answers <code>409</code>, as it did to Caio, the <code>pedidos</code> knows right away and never gets to charge anyone.</li>
  <li>The cost shows up in the math. With <code>pedidos</code>, <code>pagamentos</code> and the PSP at <strong>99.9%</strong> each, the chain only works when all three work: 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999 = <strong>99.70%</strong>. That is <strong>129 minutes per month</strong> down, against 43 minutes for a single service. With 4 hops (the <code>bff</code> joins in front), 0.999⁴ = 99.60%, or <strong>173 minutes</strong>.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">What if it fails: the PSP takes 8 seconds</h3>

<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>The <code>pagamentos</code> calls the PSP and creates the charge <code>c-7731</code>, but the PSP is slow and takes <strong>8 s</strong> to answer.</li>
  <li>The <strong>2 s</strong> timeout of the <code>pagamentos</code> (the rule from module 10) fires at 2 s. The <code>pagamentos</code> gives up, the <code>pedidos</code> gets the error and returns a failure to Davi.</li>
  <li>But the charge <code>c-7731</code> <strong>already exists at the PSP</strong>. Davi can open his banking app and pay the Pix for an order the store reported as failed.</li>
  <li>The caller does not know whether the other end did it or not. A timeout only says "I did not receive the answer in time", and that is different from "it did not happen".</li>
</ol>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li>The series math explains why a synchronous dependency is never free: each extra service in the chain takes availability away from whoever is in front.</li>
  <li>The timeout case shows that an error on the caller's side does not prove the other end did nothing. That is the gap the next lessons close, with a call back (lesson 3) and, further ahead, with idempotency (module 21).</li>
  <li>The rest of the module is a choice of format and protocol for this same call: REST, RPC, gRPC, WebSocket and GraphQL, and each one leaves the math above intact: the call still waits.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={4}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Synchronous is about the logic, not the thread</h4>
  <p>The article describes the synchronous call as "blocking". From the point of view of Davi's order that holds: the logic does not move forward until the answer arrives. But a goroutine waiting for a network response costs little (module 11), and the server serves other clients in the meantime. What blocks is the flow of the order, not the process.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Four hops and the whole chain</h4>
  <p>Each service in series multiplies the availability. With 99.9% each: 1 hop gives 43 min per month, 2 give 86 min, 3 give 129 min, 4 give 173 min and 6 give 259 min. The math assumes independent failures and equal repair time; in practice, a failure that spreads along the chain makes the result worse.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">The network is not a local function</h4>
  <p>Waldo and colleagues (1994) argue that treating a remote call as if it were local hides things that change the design: much higher latency, memory that is not shared, partial failure and concurrency. The charge that was created and nobody knows it was created is partial failure in its pure state.</p>
