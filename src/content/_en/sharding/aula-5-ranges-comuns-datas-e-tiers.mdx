<p class="ponte">In the last lesson the table was split into 3 years, and the choice of year was a <strong>range</strong>: each partition takes care of an interval of the key. It is the easiest form to understand, because the rule fits on one line. It is also the one that <strong>concentrates the most</strong>. This lesson goes through the four most common ranges in the article and measures where each one gets heavy.</p>

<OneLiner>Ranges are easy to read and to explain, but the range that receives new data, like the current year, is <strong>hot by construction</strong>.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>The range by <strong>initial</strong>. The article divides the alphabet into 6 ranges: A–E, F–J, K–N, O–R, S–V and W–Z. With the store's sample, by the initial of the name, the <strong>A–E</strong> range gets Bia, Caio and Davi (<strong>8</strong> rows) and <strong>K–N</strong> gets Lena (<strong>6</strong>). The other 4 ranges stay <strong>empty</strong>: 2 shards out of 6 do the work.</li>
  <li>The range by <strong>identifier</strong>. The 50 million ids become 3 ranges of 16.67 million. But the <code>id</code> is sequential, and <strong>100% of the new writes</strong> go to the last range. The first two stay full and idle (the "overflow"), and the last one does all the work: efficiency of 33.3%, the same math as lesson 3.</li>
  <li>The range by <strong>date</strong>. By year: 2024 has <strong>8 million</strong> rows (16 GB), 2025 has <strong>17 million</strong> (34 GB) and 2026 has <strong>25 million</strong> (50 GB), 50 million and 100 GB in total (a stated example). The current year's partition receives all the writes, so it is hot by construction, and the 2024 one is only read now and then.</li>
  <li>From there come the <strong>tiers</strong>. The 2026 partition is <strong>hot</strong>, on a fast SSD; 2025 is <strong>warm</strong>; 2024 is <strong>cold</strong>, on cheap and slow storage. The PostgreSQL documentation says rarely used data can go to cheaper media. What was a problem (the current year is hot) becomes a cost rule: old data does not need expensive disk.</li>
  <li>And <strong>retention</strong> comes free. To retire 2024, <code>DETACH</code> takes 1.1 ms and <code>DROP</code> 7.5 ms, against the 62.9 ms of <code>DELETE</code> (105,407 rows, PostgreSQL 14; lesson 4 has the table). The first two do not scan rows and leave no work for <code>VACUUM</code>.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: 1/1/2027 with no partition created</h3>

<p>Midnight of New Year's Eve has passed. The first order of 2027 tries to write and PostgreSQL answers <code>ERROR: no partition of relation "pedidos" found for row</code>. No child table covers the date. The store loses every order of the new year until someone creates the partition, and whoever wakes up to the alert finds out the error was <strong>predictable</strong>.</p>

<p>The fix is a scheduled job that creates the next period's partition <strong>before</strong> it is needed, with weeks of margin. A <code>DEFAULT</code> partition also holds the rows outside every range, but it hides the problem: it grows with nobody looking.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>A range is simple and concentrates.</strong> Initials leave 4 of 6 ranges empty; ids and dates send 100% of the writes to the last one.</li>
  <li><strong>Dates give retention.</strong> Retiring the old year is a 7.5 ms <code>DROP</code>.</li>
  <li><strong>Tiers use the imbalance.</strong> 2026 on SSD, 2025 in the middle, 2024 on cheap disk.</li>
  <li><strong>The next period's partition has to exist beforehand.</strong> Otherwise the <code>INSERT</code> fails.</li>
</WhyItMatters>
