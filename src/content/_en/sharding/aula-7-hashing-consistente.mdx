<p class="ponte">In the last lesson, going from 3 shards to 4 with <code>mod</code> moved <strong>75.2%</strong> of the rows: 75 GB to copy, 25 minutes, and most of it going between old shards. The ideal would be to move only the part the new shard will hold, 1 row in 4. The <strong>ring</strong> (hash ring) gets close to that, because it swaps the division for a search in a sorted list.</p>

<OneLiner>In consistent hashing, shards and keys live on the <strong>same circle</strong> of hashes, and each key belongs to the first shard it finds going clockwise: when a new shard joins, <strong>only its data moves</strong>, about 1 ÷ N of the total.</OneLiner>

Note the ring's 25.7% against the 75.2% of <code>mod</code>. Then switch to the <strong>Virtual nodes</strong> button and compare 1, 3, 10 and 200 points per shard.</p>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>The 32-bit hash goes from 0 to 4,294,967,295. Bend that interval into a circle, with the end glued to the start. Each <strong>shard</strong> gets positions on that circle (the hash of its name) and so does each <strong>key</strong> (the hash of the <code>cliente_id</code>). To find a key's owner, walk <strong>clockwise</strong> from it to the first shard.</li>
  <li>One point per shard leaves the slices of the circle very uneven. The fix is <strong>virtual nodes</strong> (vnodes): each shard appears at many points, the hash of <code>shard-S#0</code>, <code>shard-S#1</code> and so on. With <strong>200 points</strong> per shard (this module's value), 3 shards get <strong>35.5% / 32.2% / 32.3%</strong> of the keys, and the max ÷ mean ratio is <strong>1.07</strong>.</li>
  <li><code>shard-3</code> joins. Its 200 points fall between the neighbors' points and steal a little from each. Of the 100,000 keys, <strong>25.7%</strong> move (the ideal is 25%), and <strong>all of them to the new shard</strong>. On the 50-million-row table, that is 12.5 million, <strong>25 GB, 8 min 20 s</strong> at 50 MB/s, against the 75 GB and 25 min of <code>mod</code>.</li>
  <li>With the store's 5 customers, only <code>caio-58</code> (shard-0 → shard-3) and <code>davi-12</code> (shard-2 → shard-3) move. <code>lena-77</code> stays on shard-0, <code>bia-31</code> and <code>mkt-01</code> on shard-2. The ring's shard numbers are not the <code>mod 3</code> ones from lesson 6: it is another spreading function, with another map.</li>
  <li>Virtual nodes do more than balance: they spread the <strong>impact of a departure</strong>. If the biggest shard leaves a ring with 1 point per shard, all of its keys (70.2%) go to <em>one</em> neighbor, which now has <strong>91.2%</strong>: the domino effect. With 200 points, the keys split between the two neighbors.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<p>With few points the distribution is lopsided and the fraction that moves drifts from the ideal (from 15.2% to 32.1%). With 200, it is close to 1/4. With 100 points the max ÷ mean ratio goes up a little (1.18) by chance: the luck of the points matters less the more points there are, but it does not disappear. On every row, the keys that move go only to the new shard.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Links to modules 7 and 13</h3>

<p>Module 7 already used this idea: when <code>catalogo-3</code> leaves, the balancer with <code>% 2</code> moves 2/3 of the customers to another replica, and consistent hashing only 1/3. The Kafka from module 13 does <strong>not</strong> use a ring: it uses <code>murmur2 mod N</code>. That is why, going from 3 to 6 partitions, orders <code>1049</code> and <code>1051</code> move from p0 to p3, exactly the "doubling" math: about 50%.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: few points and the domino</h3>

<p>The store builds the ring with 1 point per shard, to save memory. The distribution comes out <strong>70.2 / 8.8 / 21.0</strong>: the first shard holds 7 of every 10 rows and the second almost nothing. With the lab's 3 points, <code>Shard-00</code> ended up with no tenant at all. Then the biggest shard goes down and leaves the ring. With 1 point, the 70.2% go <em>all</em> to one neighbor, which now has <strong>91.2%</strong> and saturates: the domino. With 3 points, 56.7 thousand of the 59.0 thousand keys go to <code>shard-1</code>, which ends up with 74.4%. With 200 points, <code>shard-1</code> gets 53.1% and <code>shard-2</code> 46.9%: they share it.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Only the new shard's data moves.</strong> 25.7% of the keys (8 min 20 s) instead of 75.2% (25 min).</li>
  <li><strong>The keys all go to the new shard.</strong> None swaps places between old shards.</li>
  <li><strong>Virtual nodes balance and protect.</strong> 1 point: ratio 2.11; 200 points: 1.07. And they spread the impact of a departure.</li>
  <li><strong>The ring has no state,</strong> but it needs all the points in memory, and the router code has to be the same everywhere.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={5}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Other ways to get to the same place</h4>
  <p><strong>Jump Consistent Hash</strong> (Google, 2014) uses no ring and no memory: it computes the shard with a short loop and only works if the shards are numbered 0 to N − 1 (you can grow N, but not remove one from the middle). <strong>Rendezvous hashing</strong> (HRW) gives each key a score for every shard and picks the one with the highest score; lesson 9 uses that idea to choose replicas. Amazon's Dynamo popularized the ring with virtual nodes.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the source article and the lab</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>"The module's values change very little."</strong> The ring does not use a modulo: it uses sorted positions on a circle.</li>
    <li><strong>3 virtual nodes.</strong> The code has 3 per shard (59 / 18 / 23% in a simulation, and <code>Shard-00</code> is born empty). In practice it is tens to hundreds (200 here: 35 / 32 / 32%).</li>
    <li><strong>"Only the data between the new node and the next one changes."</strong> That holds <em>per virtual node</em>: with several points, the new shard receives a little from each neighbor (and only it receives).</li>
    <li><strong>The lab does not move data.</strong> <code>RemoveNode</code> and <code>AddNode</code> only change the ring; the <code>// Nova movimentação</code> in the article's output is a manual annotation. With an empty ring, <code>GetTenantNode</code> panics.</li>
  </ul>
