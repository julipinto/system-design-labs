<p class="ponte">In the last lesson the <code>pedidos</code> table became 3 servers on paper, and the price showed up right away: reports in 3 parts and transactions that cross shards. Is it worth paying? The answer has a good side and a bad side, both with numbers. The good side is <strong>capacity, space and isolation</strong>. The bad side is <strong>queries without the key, tail latency and more incidents</strong>.</p>

<OneLiner>Sharding buys capacity, space and isolation because each server handles one piece; it charges queries without the key, tail latency and more chances that some shard is down.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>The first gain is <strong>capacity</strong>. Each shard has the ceiling of 150 orders per second from module 14, so 3 shards add up to <strong>3 × 150 = 450 orders/s</strong>, on paper. The math only works if traffic spreads evenly, and lesson 3 shows when it does not.</li>
  <li>The second is <strong>space</strong> and maintenance time. The 140 GB become <strong>46.7 GB per shard</strong>. The backup of the three runs in parallel and takes <strong>15 min 33 s</strong>, against 47 min on the single server. Each shard's index is also smaller, so the search reads less disk.</li>
  <li>Then comes the query. With the key, the router computes the shard and asks <strong>just one</strong>. Without the key, it asks <strong>all of them</strong> and merges the answers (<strong>scatter-gather</strong>), and the answer takes as long as the slowest of the N.</li>
  <li>That is why the tail matters. In <em>The Tail at Scale</em>, Dean and Barroso show that, for a query that waits on N shards, the chance that at least one answers above its p99 is 1 − 0.99<sup>N</sup>: <strong>2.97%</strong> with 3 shards, <strong>9.56%</strong> with 10, <strong>39.5%</strong> with 50 and <strong>63.4%</strong> with 100. Each shard's p99 becomes the query's <strong>p97</strong> with 3.</li>
  <li>And availability drops. With each shard at 99.9%, the chance that <strong>some</strong> shard is down is 1 − 0.999<sup>N</sup>: <strong>0.10%</strong> with 1, <strong>0.2997%</strong> with 3, <strong>0.9955%</strong> with 10 and <strong>4.88%</strong> with 50. For <em>one customer</em> the chance stays at 0.1%. For the <em>team</em>, incidents multiply.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: the report on 50 shards</h3>

<p>Imagine the store a few years from now, with 50 shards, and a report that sums the month's orders without the customer key. The query goes to all 50. <strong>39.5% of the time</strong> at least one of them answers above its p99, and the whole report waits for it. The report gets slow without any shard having a problem that an alarm would catch.</p>

<p>What to do: give the query the key whenever possible, send reports to a read copy or to a data warehouse, and use <em>hedged requests</em> (ask a second replica when the first one is slow), a technique from the same Dean and Barroso article.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Capacity:</strong> 3 × 150 = 450 orders/s on paper, if the load spreads out.</li>
  <li><strong>Space:</strong> 46.7 GB per shard and a 15 min 33 s backup in parallel.</li>
  <li><strong>A query with the key reads 1 shard.</strong> Without the key it reads all of them and waits for the slowest.</li>
  <li><strong>The tail grows with N:</strong> 2.97% with 3 shards and 39.5% with 50.</li>
  <li><strong>Some shard down:</strong> 0.30% with 3 and 4.88% with 50 (each customer stays at 0.1%).</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <p>Both columns use the same formula, 1 − p<sup>N</sup>, with p = 0.999 and p = 0.99. They assume the shards fail and slow down independently, which is a simplification: a bad deploy or a network problem takes several down at once.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Correction to the source article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>"If a node fails, the other shards stay available."</strong> The article states this as an advantage. It is true only for the keys that live in the other shards, and only if the failure stays isolated. The chance that <em>some</em> shard goes down grows with N. And, without replicas (module 17), the data of the shard that went down becomes unavailable.</li>
  </ul>
