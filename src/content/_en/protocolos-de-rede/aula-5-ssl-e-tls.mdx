<p class="ponte">TCP delivers the whole photo in order, but in the clear: on public Wi-Fi, anyone on the same network reads everything. In lesson 2, layer 6 turned the photo into noise. Here is how client and server agree on the key to that noise without anyone in the middle finding it.</p>

<OneLiner>TLS (the successor to SSL) encrypts the conversation to guarantee privacy and integrity, and its handshake exists to arrive at a unique session key.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>First, client and server <strong>negotiate</strong> the version and algorithms.</li>
  <li>Then the server proves who it is with a <strong>certificate</strong>, and the client checks it.</li>
  <li>With the server authenticated, the two derive a symmetric <strong>session key</strong>, without sending it over the wire.</li>
  <li>Only then does that key encrypt the rest of the conversation: <strong>asymmetric for the negotiation, symmetric for the data</strong>.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<p>The price of privacy is the handshake. In the figure's example, with an RTT of 80 ms, the request goes out after 240 ms on TLS 1.2 and 160 ms on TLS 1.3, counting TCP too.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li>Each handshake RTT is time before the first useful byte.</li>
  <li>That's why TLS 1.3, reused connections and TLS terminated close to the user (CDN, load balancer at the edge) lower latency.</li>
  <li>An expired certificate is a classic cause of downtime.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={1}>
  <p>Today the versions in use are <strong>TLS 1.2</strong> and <strong>TLS 1.3</strong>. 1.3 is more secure and has a faster handshake: 1 round trip instead of 2.</p>
  <p>On session resumption, 1.3 also allows 0-RTT (with security caveats, such as replay).</p>
