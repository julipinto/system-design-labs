<p class="ponte">Lena's phone already knows how to address, deliver and encrypt, but all of that starts from an IP, and she tapped a link with a name. DNS is what turns the name into an IP.</p>

<OneLiner>DNS is the internet's phone book: it turns <code>demo.fidelissauro.dev</code> into an IP address by asking servers that get more and more specific.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<p>People remember names, machines need IPs. Before opening any connection, the phone asks the <strong>resolver</strong> (usually the provider's, or 8.8.8.8, 1.1.1.1), which runs this sequence of questions:</p>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Cache:</strong> the browser, the system and the resolver check whether they already know the answer.</li>
  <li>If nobody knows, the resolver starts at the top. The <strong>root server</strong> doesn't know the IP, but it knows who handles <code>.dev</code>.</li>
  <li>The <strong>.dev TLD server</strong> doesn't know the IP either, but it knows who the name server for <code>fidelissauro.dev</code> is.</li>
  <li>The <strong>authoritative server</strong> has the final word and answers with the IP of <code>demo.fidelissauro.dev</code>.</li>
  <li>The resolver returns the answer and <strong>caches it</strong> for the TTL. Only then does the browser connect to the IP.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<p>Four servers sound like a lot, but the cache makes the lookup cheap: in the figure's example, about 85 ms with no cache, 5 ms with the resolver's cache and 0 ms with the browser's cache.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li>DNS is the first step of every request and also a single point of failure.</li>
  <li>The <strong>TTL</strong> is the balance: high means fewer lookups but a slower failover; low switches IPs fast but generates more lookups.</li>
  <li>Besides finding the IP, DNS is used for load balancing and routing by region.</li>
</WhyItMatters>
