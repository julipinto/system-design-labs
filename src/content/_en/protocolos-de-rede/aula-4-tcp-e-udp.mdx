<p class="ponte">IP takes each of the 215 slices to the right machine, but it guarantees nothing: a slice can vanish, arrive duplicated or arrive out of order. TCP takes care of that, at layer 4, unless the application chooses UDP.</p>

<OneLiner>TCP is the courier who demands a signature and confirms every package; UDP is the one who slides the mail under the gate and moves on to the next.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<p>To see this up close, the figure shows only the first 3 slices of Lena's photo, 100 bytes each (300 bytes), instead of the real 215 slices of ~1,460 bytes; the seq and ack numbers are examples (in TCP, each slice is called a segment).</p>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>TCP</strong> starts by opening the connection, with the <strong>three-way handshake</strong>: SYN, SYN-ACK, ACK (1 RTT).</li>
  <li>Once the connection is open, it transfers <strong>numbered segments</strong>. The receiver reorders what arrives out of order and sends ACK.</li>
  <li>Whatever isn't acknowledged in time is <strong>retransmitted</strong>.</li>
  <li>When it's done, it closes with the <strong>four-way close</strong>: each side sends FIN and gets an ACK.</li>
  <li><strong>UDP</strong> skips all of that: it sends independent datagrams, with no connection, no order, no acknowledgment. Fast and light.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<p>Each TCP guarantee is one more message, and each message waits for a round trip.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li>The handshake is a fixed cost per connection, which is why <strong>keep-alive</strong> and connection pools exist.</li>
  <li>Retransmission explains latency spikes when there is loss.</li>
  <li>When choosing, data that loses value fast (voice, a player's position in a game) goes over UDP, and data that can't go missing (payments, databases) goes over TCP.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={4}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">UDP: no connection, no guarantees</h4>
  <p>UDP has almost no bureaucracy, so it's fast and light. It suits what tolerates loss or where old data is worth nothing: voice and video calls, online games, DNS, NTP.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">TCP: connection-oriented</h4>
  <p>TCP opens, maintains, verifies and closes the connection. It delivers intact and in order.</p>
  <p>In the handshake, each side picks an <strong>ISN</strong> (Initial Sequence Number) and the other confirms it. The ACK is cumulative: "I received everything up to here, I expect number X".</p>
  <p>TCP's reliability costs bureaucracy, and that's why it's slower than UDP. Even so, most service protocols use TCP.</p>

  <p class="hdr-key">In blue, what the two share: ports and checksum. TCP spends the rest on controlling order, acknowledgment and window.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Flow and congestion</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Flow control (sliding window):</strong> the receiver reports how many bytes still fit in its buffer, and the sender doesn't send more than that.</li>
    <li><strong>Congestion control:</strong> the sender also limits what it sends by the estimated capacity of the network: it starts slow and speeds up as long as there is no loss.</li>
  </ul>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Other transports</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>SCTP:</strong> messages with <em>multi-streaming</em> (several independent streams in the same association) and <em>multihoming</em> (several IPs per endpoint, switching if one goes down).</li>
    <li><strong>DCCP:</strong> similar to UDP, with no delivery guarantee, but with congestion control. Barely used.</li>
    <li><strong>QUIC:</strong> runs over UDP and bundles connection, independent streams and TLS 1.3. It's the basis of HTTP/3 (lesson 8).</li>
  </ul>
