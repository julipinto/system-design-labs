<p class="ponte">In the last lesson PSP-B took one SPoF off the path, but the DNS and the certificate did not even show up in the diagram. The problem with a hidden SPoF is that <strong>nobody looks at it</strong>. So the first task is to look at the whole path, link by link, and ask each one who takes over if it disappears.</p>

<OneLiner>Finding a SPoF means <strong>drawing the happy path</strong> of each critical function, <strong>listing every actor</strong> (vendors, certificates and people included) and asking each one: <strong>who takes over if you disappear, and how fast?</strong></OneLiner>

<p>Five links count as SPoF in the math: <strong>1, 2, 3, 4 and 12</strong>. Link 10 is <strong>partial</strong>: the passive takes over, but in 35 s and with 0.3 s of data missing (lessons 4 and 5 tell what that costs). Link 12 is <strong>off the path</strong> of the order, and that is why it weighs less than it seems.</p>

<h3 class="sec">The math in series and in parallel</h3>

<p>Links in series multiply their availabilities (module 21). Duplicated links form a parallel, which only fails if <strong>both</strong> copies fail: 1 − 0.001² = 99.9999%. The table compares three worlds:</p>

<div class="sr-tablewrap">
<table class="sr-table">
  <thead><tr><th>Scenario</th><th>Math</th><th>Availability</th><th>Minutes of failure per month</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><th>12 links in series, nothing duplicated</th><td>0.999<sup>12</sup></td><td><strong>98.8066%</strong></td><td><strong>515.6</strong></td></tr>
    <tr><th>5 SPoFs, the other 7 duplicated</th><td>0.999<sup>5</sup> × 0.999999<sup>7</sup></td><td><strong>99.5003%</strong></td><td><strong>215.9</strong></td></tr>
    <tr><th>all 12 duplicated</th><td>0.999999<sup>12</sup></td><td><strong>99.9988%</strong></td><td><strong>0.52</strong></td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<p>Of the <strong>215.9</strong> minutes left in the second scenario, <strong>215.6</strong> come from the five SPoFs. The seven duplicated links are worth <strong>0.3</strong> minute. Each SPoF fixed gives back about <strong>43.2 min per month</strong> when all of them are worth 99.9%. In practice, what separates one from another is the <strong>cost</strong> of the fix and the <strong>blast radius</strong>.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What is not a SPoF, and the ones that are not technical</h3>

<p>Not every "only one" is a SPoF. The mesh control plane (module 10) sits in one region, but the sidecars <strong>keep running on the last configuration</strong> if it disappears. The same goes for the <code>edge-loja</code> map (link 12): the edges keep the <strong>last map</strong> and keep routing. That is <strong>static stability</strong>: what sits on the order's path does not depend on something that only exists to change the configuration.</p>

<p>And there are SPoFs that no technical diagram shows. The <strong>certificate</strong> renewed by hand once a year is one (link 2): on the day the deadline passes and nobody remembers, HTTPS goes down for everyone. The fix is <strong>automatic renewal</strong> and an alert 30 days ahead. Another is the person: "only Davi knows how to promote the database". An organizational SPoF fails when Davi is on vacation.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Where to start: radius × probability × cost</h3>

<p>With five SPoFs in hand you cannot fix everything at once. Each one gets a score on three questions: how much damage it does (<strong>radius</strong>), how likely it is to fail (<strong>probability</strong>) and how much it costs to fix. The certificate has total radius, known probability (the expiry date) and a <strong>cheap</strong> fix: automate it. It goes first. Identity in two regions has high radius and an expensive fix: it goes into a plan, with the gateway caching the keys for 24 h until then.</p>

<h3 class="sec">If it goes wrong: the audit ages</h3>

<p>Yesterday's SPoF list does not hold for today's store. When module 26 created the cells, <code>edge-loja</code> and the map were born, two links that were not in the previous list. An audit done <strong>before</strong> module 26 and filed away would not have them on the list, and the store would keep believing the routing was the usual one.</p>

<p>The flow changes whenever the architecture changes. That is why the audit is a <strong>continuous review</strong>, redone at every change to the order path, and not a project with an end date.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Draw the whole happy path.</strong> The store's five SPoFs were at the edges: DNS, certificate, identity, the map and <code>edge-loja</code>.</li>
  <li><strong>Series kills.</strong> Twelve links at 99.9% give 98.8066%, or 515.6 min per month.</li>
  <li><strong>Five links are worth almost everything.</strong> Of the 215.9 min left, 215.6 belong to the SPoFs; duplicating them takes it to 0.52.</li>
  <li><strong>Not every "only one" is a SPoF, and not every SPoF is technical.</strong> A control plane off the path is static stability; the hand-renewed certificate and the single person are SPoFs.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <h4 class="deep-h">Prioritization and corrections to the source article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Independence.</strong> The 99.5003% and 99.9988% math only holds with <strong>independent failures</strong>. <code>edge-loja</code> with the same code in all three instances has <strong>one</strong> common failure; the fix is not a fourth instance, it is publishing in <strong>rings</strong> and keeping a "last good" map.</li>
    <li><strong>The equal 99.9% values are an example.</strong> In practice each link has its own availability (use the widget). What the math shows is the <strong>shape</strong>: series drops fast, parallel rises fast.</li>
    <li><strong>The article talks about mapping, but gives no math.</strong> Series and parallel come from module 21. The article also leaves out <strong>static stability</strong> and organizational SPoFs (certificate, person).</li>
    <li><strong>What was not run.</strong> The copies and times of each link are the brief's example; the math was computed in Python on 10/21/2026.</li>
