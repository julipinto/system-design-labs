<p class="ponte">In module 26 the store became four cells, each with the full stack and a <strong>passive cell</strong> in another region. The poison from <code>mkt-01</code> took down <strong>one</strong> cell and Lena still bought order <strong>1060</strong>. But a bigger question stayed open: what if it is not one cell that falls, but <strong>the whole São Paulo region</strong>? Before answering, we need to know what exactly takes everything down when it fails.</p>

<OneLiner>A <strong>single point of failure</strong> (SPoF) is any component whose failure takes down <strong>all or part</strong> of the system <strong>with no alternative path</strong>. Removing every one is impossible and duplicating everything is waste: the goal is to shrink the <strong>blast radius</strong>, the <strong>surprise</strong> and the <strong>time to recover</strong>.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>The bridge.</strong> A city with a single bridge: if the bridge falls, you can still arrive by boat, but <strong>not everyone</strong>, and what goes in and out of the city is badly hurt.</li>
  <li><strong>The damage has a size.</strong> When a SPoF fails, the system goes into <strong>degraded mode</strong> (at best) or <strong>total outage</strong> (at worst).</li>
  <li><strong>The heavier it is, the more it breaks.</strong> The bigger the component's responsibility, the bigger the damage of losing it without a <em>fallback</em> flow.</li>
  <li><strong>Component × product.</strong> A SPoF takes down <strong>one service</strong>. A disaster (lesson 4) takes down the product, a region or a provider.</li>
  <li><strong>Fixing takes time.</strong> Recovering by hand or rebuilding a component is slow and can lose data, so the good fix happens <strong>before</strong>.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">The bridge and PSP-A</h3>

<p>The city with one bridge is the article's image, and it serves once. In the store, the bridge has a name: it is <code>pagamentos</code> talking to <strong>PSP-A</strong>, the payment provider. If PSP-A disappears and there is no other, Lena's order gets to the end of the road and <strong>cannot close</strong>. The catalog still opens, the cart still saves, but nobody buys: it is <strong>degraded mode</strong>, with the store as a shop window.</p>

<p>With <strong>PSP-B</strong> from module 21 turned on, <code>pagamentos</code> switches provider and Lena does not even notice. PSP-A stopped being a SPoF <strong>without</strong> anyone duplicating the whole system: it was enough to duplicate <strong>the point</strong> where the failure hurt.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Total × partial: the blast radius</h3>

<p>The article talks about <strong>total or partial</strong> unavailability, and the difference changes what the store does next. In the table, each failure with what it leaves standing:</p>

<p>What separates the rows is <strong>responsibility</strong>: <code>edge-loja</code> is on the path of <strong>every</strong> order, and one <code>pedidos</code> pod is on the path of a third. That is why the same kind of failure costs much more in one place than in the other.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What the store already removed, and what it does not know it has</h3>

<p>After 26 modules the store has removed several SPoFs without calling them by name:</p>

<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><code>pagamentos</code> has <strong>PSP-B</strong> (module 21).</li>
  <li>The services run on <strong>3 pods</strong>, behind a load balancer that drops whoever fails.</li>
  <li>The database was split into <strong>shards</strong> (module 16) and then into <strong>cells</strong>, and the blast radius of a failure got small.</li>
  <li>Each cell has a <strong>passive</strong> (module 26) holding a copy of the data.</li>
  <li>The router, <code>edge-loja</code>, runs on <strong>3 instances</strong>: SP, Rio and Recife.</li>
</ol>

<p>All of these are solved <strong>where someone already looked</strong>. The problem is the rest: a DNS that depends on a single provider, a certificate someone renews by hand once a year. None of them shows up in the architecture diagram, and that is why they cause surprises. Finding them is the subject of the next lesson.</p>

<h3 class="sec">If it goes wrong: redundancy that is not independent</h3>

<p><code>edge-loja</code> has three instances, so it looks protected. But all three run the <strong>same code</strong> and read the <strong>same map</strong> of cells. When a bad map publication arrives, <strong>all three</strong> apply the error and all three fail together. It is a <strong>common-mode</strong> failure: one cause, three outages.</p>

<p>The math shows how big the illusion is. Three independent instances at 99.9% would give 1 − 0.001³ = <strong>99.9999999%</strong>. With a shared cause, the real availability of the set is that of <strong>one</strong> instance: <strong>99.9%</strong>. Duplicating something with the same defect does not help.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>SPoF is a question, not a type of part.</strong> Database, load balancer, gateway, broker, another service, a vendor: anything with no alternative path.</li>
  <li><strong>The damage depends on the weight.</strong> PSP-A without PSP-B leaves the store as a shop window; the whole <code>edge-loja</code> down is a total outage.</li>
  <li><strong>Duplicating the point is enough.</strong> PSP-B solved PSP-A without duplicating the store.</li>
  <li><strong>Redundancy has to be independent.</strong> Three instances with the same map are one failure: 99.9999999% on paper, 99.9% in practice.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={4}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the source article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>"Duplicate" assumes independent failures.</strong> Two parts at 99.9% in parallel give 99.9999% <strong>only</strong> if they share no cause. The same code, the same map, the same region or the same DNS vendor <strong>add</strong> the failure instead of multiplying it. The article talks about simultaneous failures without using the idea of <strong>common mode</strong>.</li>
    <li><strong>Not everything is a component.</strong> The article treats SPoF at the component level and disaster at the product level. The split is useful, but the border is <strong>scope of damage</strong>, and lesson 4 shows a SPoF (the <strong>hub</strong>) that only appears when you look at the region.</li>
    <li><strong>What was not run.</strong> No SPoF was actually brought down in this module: the times and the math come from the brief's model, flagged as an example.</li>
  </ul>
