<p class="ponte">Last lesson <code>e-502</code> stopped counting twice, and the list of Event Sourcing precautions is complete: expected version, publication that does not lose events, projections that can be redone, rehydration with snapshot, ordering by version, idempotency. Each one cost a lesson. What is left is adding up the bill and deciding whether, for the store, <strong>it is worth paying</strong>.</p>

<OneLiner>Event Sourcing gives <strong>history, auditing, rebuilding and projections on demand</strong>, and charges <strong>complexity</strong>: versioned events, eventual consistency, one projection for everything you query and idempotency at every endpoint. It is worth it where <strong>the history is the product</strong>.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>What you gain.</strong> Every change is stored, in order, and can be <strong>audited</strong> ("what was 1047 at 14:15?") and <strong>rebuilt</strong> (a corrupted read model is redone from the Store, in 2 h 47 min for the 200 million events). New projections are born by reading the past.</li>
  <li><strong>What you pay.</strong> <strong>Versioned</strong> events (the format evolves with <em>upcasting</em>), <strong>eventual consistency</strong> between the Store and the models, <strong>one projection</strong> for each thing you query, <strong>idempotency</strong> in every consumer and a <strong>concurrency</strong> strategy per stream.</li>
  <li><strong>When to use it.</strong> In domains where <strong>the history is the product</strong>: payments, balances, orders with auditing, medical records. When you need to <strong>rebuild</strong> state. When the team has maturity with events.</li>
  <li><strong>When not to use it.</strong> Simple records, CRUD, an entity that rarely changes and whose history nobody reads, and a team with no practice with events. Fowler and Microsoft point to cases like these as low-gain for the pattern.</li>
  <li><strong>What it combines with.</strong> Event Sourcing is <strong>not required</strong> for CQRS or for Saga, but they combine. The saga with the log as its source (module 19) and CQRS with the Store on the command side (module 18).</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">What changes in the store</h3>

<p>Order 1047 becomes the list of events <code>e-501</code>, <code>e-502</code>, <code>e-504</code> and <code>e-505</code>, in the <code>pedido-1047</code> stream. The <code>pedidos</code> table stops being the <strong>source</strong> and becomes a <strong>projection</strong>: a read model you can delete and redo. What does not change is Lena's screen, which keeps reading a ready-made row.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: personal data in an immutable log</h3>

<p>1047's <code>PedidoCriado</code> carries Lena's address. One day she asks the store to <strong>delete her data</strong>. But the event is immutable, and the log is the source of truth: it is not edited. There are two paths people usually talk about. One is to <strong>not put personal data in the event</strong> (the event only holds a reference, and the data stays in another table that can be deleted). The other is to <strong>encrypt the personal data per customer</strong> and discard the key when the deletion request arrives, so the event is still there and the data became unreadable. This module <strong>only warns</strong>: there is no verified source here on how each law requires deletion, and the design has to be thought through <strong>before</strong> the first event is written, because afterwards it is too late.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>It is a trade-off, not an upgrade.</strong> The store trades simplicity for history and rebuilding.</li>
  <li><strong>It is worth it where the history is the product.</strong> Payments, balances, orders with auditing and medical records.</li>
  <li><strong>It is not worth it for CRUD.</strong> Simple records and a team with no practice with events pay the bill without getting the gain.</li>
  <li><strong>It combines, but does not oblige.</strong> CQRS and Saga work with or without Event Sourcing.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<p class="gancho">The store now keeps the facts, separates reading from writing and compensates for what goes wrong. But all of that depends on <strong>calls that can be slow, repeat or fail</strong>: the PSP answering in 8 s, the duplicated event, the storm of retries, the <em>fallback</em>. What is left is how the store <strong>stays up</strong> when dependencies fail: that is module 21, Resilience Patterns.</p>
