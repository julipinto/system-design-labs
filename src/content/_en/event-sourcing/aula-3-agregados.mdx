<p class="ponte">Last lesson left a list that only grows, and a question: who stops anything from getting into it? A <code>PedidoEnviado</code> for an order that has not even been paid would be a <strong>false</strong> fact, and since the event is immutable, the falsehood would stay forever. Someone has to check the rule <strong>before</strong> the fact exists. That someone is called the <strong>aggregate</strong>.</p>

<OneLiner>The <strong>aggregate</strong> is the unit that <strong>decides</strong>, <strong>validates</strong> and <strong>orders</strong> the events of one subject: a <em>stream</em>, a consistency boundary. It receives <strong>commands</strong> (imperative, can be refused) and emits <strong>events</strong> (past tense, always true).</OneLiner>

<DoIt>
  <p>In the figure, in the <strong>Aggregate</strong> scenario, send the <code>Enviar</code> command while the order is still <code>criado</code> and watch the <strong>refusal</strong>: no event is born. Then send <code>Pagar</code> and then <code>Enviar</code>, and watch the <code>PedidoEnviado</code> go onto the tape with the next version.</p>
</DoIt>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Two aggregates in the store.</strong> The <code>Pedido</code> (stream <code>pedido-&lt;id&gt;</code>, in the <code>pedidos</code> service) and the <code>Estoque</code> (stream <code>estoque-&lt;sku&gt;</code>). Each has its own internal state, its own rules and its own queue of events.</li>
  <li><strong>The command asks.</strong> <code>Pagar</code>, <code>Enviar</code>, <code>Reservar</code> are imperatives: they say what you <strong>want</strong> to happen. They can be refused. Nobody writes anything just because it was asked.</li>
  <li><strong>The rule decides.</strong> The <code>Pedido</code> accepts <code>Pagar</code> only in <code>criado</code> and <code>Enviar</code> only in <code>pago</code>. The <code>Estoque</code> accepts <code>Reservar</code> while the balance is above 0 and <strong>refuses</strong> at a balance of 0. To decide, the aggregate first rebuilds its state from its own events (the rehydration of lesson 7).</li>
  <li><strong>The event records.</strong> Once the command is accepted, a fact in the past is born: <code>PedidoPago</code>, <code>PedidoEnviado</code>, <code>EstoqueReservado</code>. It goes at the end of the stream with the next version and <strong>never changes again</strong>.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">Whose fact is it</h3>

<p>One design choice is worth noting, because it decides where each event lives. <code>PedidoPago</code> is a fact about the <strong>order</strong>: the <code>pedidos</code> service records it when <code>pagamentos</code> notifies it, and keeps the <code>evento_id</code> <code>e-502</code> in the <strong>metadata</strong>. <code>EstoqueReservado</code> is a fact about <strong>stock</strong>: it goes into the <code>estoque-GATO-042</code> stream as version 2 (version 1 is the initial balance, +3). That is why order 1047 has 4 events in its stream, not 5.</p>

<p>The size of the aggregate is a design decision: <strong>small</strong>. One order, one SKU. Never "the store". The reason shows up below.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: the aggregate that is too big</h3>

<p>Suppose all of the store's stock is <strong>one</strong> aggregate, with a single stream. On Black Friday the store gets 30 orders per second, and each one needs a <code>Reservar</code>: that is <strong>30 commands per second on the same stream</strong>. Each command reads the state, decides and tries to write the next version, and two at the same time fight for the <strong>same</strong> version (the conflict from lesson 4). The stream turns into a queue. The fix is one stream <strong>per SKU</strong>: the cat (<code>estoque-GATO-042</code>) and the mug (<code>estoque-CANECA-007</code>) stop fighting.</p>

<p>There is a second, quieter failure: the <strong>event that lies</strong>. If the command goes through the wrong rule (say, an <code>Enviar</code> accepted while the order is in <code>criado</code>), the <code>PedidoEnviado</code> is born, is "true" for everyone and is <strong>permanent</strong>. Fixing it takes another event (lesson 2). That is why the rule lives <strong>inside</strong> the aggregate, before the fact.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>The aggregate protects the truth of the log.</strong> Only events the rule accepted get in; a refused one leaves no trace in the stream.</li>
  <li><strong>Command and event are different.</strong> The command is imperative and can fail; the event is past tense and never fails.</li>
  <li><strong>One stream, one consistency boundary.</strong> Inside it, order and rule are guaranteed; across streams, they are not.</li>
  <li><strong>Small is better.</strong> One SKU per stream: 30 commands/s get spread out instead of landing in one queue.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <h4 class="deep-h">What the article says and what we add</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>The definition.</strong> The article describes the aggregate as the unit "that decides which events can occur, in what order and under which conditions". That is right. Two things are missing: the <strong>size</strong> (a big aggregate becomes a bottleneck, as above) and the separation between <strong>command</strong> (can fail) and <strong>event</strong> (fact). Fowler defines the domain event as the record of something that affects the domain and <strong>already happened</strong>.</li>

<p class="gancho">The aggregate decides and orders, and the result is a sequence of events with versions. What is left is <strong>where</strong> those events live, and what stops two commands from writing the same version at the same time.</p>
