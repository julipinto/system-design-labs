<p class="ponte">In the last lesson the shop was left with five ways to stand a mesh up, and all of them charge something: code, CPU, memory or an extra proxy in the path. What remains is the question that comes before choosing the way: does the shop need a mesh? Six services in three languages are close to the limit, and what decides it is what you stand to lose without one.</p>

<OneLiner>A mesh is worth it when many services, in several languages, need the same network rules and mTLS, and there is a team to operate it; with few services and a single team, a library and a good gateway do the job, and the mesh is just one more system to maintain.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Many services.</strong> Each new service increases the number of pairs that call each other, and each pair needs a timeout, a retry and a metric. The mesh writes the rule once; without it, the rule is copied into each service and gets old in each one.</li>
  <li><strong>Several languages.</strong> This is the shop's case: Go, Node.js and Java, with three network libraries that need to behave the same way. With a single language, a shared library already solves a good part. With three, the mesh gains a point.</li>
  <li><strong>mTLS everywhere.</strong> If an audit requires every call between pods to be encrypted and each side to prove who it is, <code>estoque</code> with no TLS stops being an oversight and becomes a problem. The sidecar or ztunnel delivers that to everyone at once, and the control plane rotates the 24 h certificates with nobody remembering.</li>
  <li><strong>A team to operate it.</strong> The mesh is a system: it needs someone to update the control plane, swap Envoy, read the YAML and know why a call came back 403. Without that team, the gain turns into debt.</li>
  <li><strong>When it is not worth it.</strong> Three services, one language and one team: a retry and timeout library, TLS configured at the gateway and metrics in the app itself cost less than a control plane plus a proxy per pod. The mesh can wait for the day the rule starts to diverge between teams.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">When it fails: the mesh goes down too</h3>

<p>Everything the mesh does for everyone, it also gets wrong for everyone. In the shop, a team applies <code>PeerAuthentication</code> in <code>STRICT</code> mode on the <code>loja</code> namespace: from then on, each pod only accepts calls encrypted with mTLS. A pod that came up without a sidecar, like <code>relatorios</code> before the injection reached it, keeps sending plain text, and its calls start to be refused. Another team copies the retry rule from lesson 2 to all three hops and the shop is back to 27 calls to <code>pagamentos</code>. And if <code>istiod</code> goes down, the calls keep going, but the 24 h certificates expire without rotation. The mesh takes code away from the teams and moves the risk to one place, and that place is where someone needs to be watching.</p>

<p>When it is time to choose a mesh, the market offers more than one path. Each gives a different answer to the question from the last lesson: where the proxy lives.</p>

<p>One absence deserves a note: <strong>AWS App Mesh</strong>, which used to show up in lists of meshes, had its end of support announced by AWS for September 30, 2026. After that date, the console and the resources are no longer accessible. Whoever used it has to migrate, and whoever is choosing now should not count on it.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>A decision with numbers.</strong> Instead of "microservices call for a mesh", you count how many services, languages and teams exist and whether mTLS is required.</li>
  <li><strong>Starting small is allowed.</strong> Turning on L4 only (mTLS and policy by identity, as in the ambient of lesson 5) already delivers what weighs the most, without paying for L7 everywhere.</li>
  <li><strong>The cost is operations, not only CPU.</strong> The 3 vCPU and 1.5 GB of the 30 sidecars are the smallest of the prices; the biggest is keeping the control plane, the YAML and the updates.</li>
  <li><strong>The way out exists.</strong> A library plus a gateway remain an honest answer when the shop is small.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={5}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Questions to ask before adopting</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>How many calls between services are there today, and how many languages write them?</li>
    <li>Does anyone require mTLS and identity per service, and who will prove that to the auditor?</li>
    <li>Who updates the control plane and Envoy, and how long does each update take with the 30 pods?</li>
    <li>Is L4 enough (mTLS, policy by identity) or does the shop need retry and canary in the proxy?</li>
    <li>What is the plan if the mesh goes down, and do the calls keep going with the last configuration?</li>
  </ul>

<p class="gancho">The mesh takes care of the conversation between the services: who calls whom, with which rule and encrypted. But inside <code>pedidos</code>, the program serves hundreds of calls at the same time, and the mesh sees none of that. What is left is how a program does several things at once without one stepping on another: that is the topic of the next module, Concurrency and Parallelism.</p>
