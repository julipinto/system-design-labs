<p class="ponte">In the last lesson CDC took the dual write out of the code, but the event came out as a changed <strong>row</strong>, and the store wants the whole <code>PedidoCriado</code>, with items and price at the time. The way out is to write the <strong>event as data</strong>, in a table of the same database, in the <strong>same transaction</strong> as the order. If the order goes in, the event goes in; if the transaction rolls back, both disappear.</p>

<OneLiner>The <strong>transactional outbox</strong> writes the event to a <strong>table in the same database, in the same transaction</strong> as the order: either both go in or neither does. A second process (the <strong>relay</strong>) takes the table to the broker, with <strong>at-least-once</strong> delivery, which is why the consumer has to be <strong>idempotent</strong>.</OneLiner>

  <p>In the figure, in the <strong>Outbox</strong> scenario, run the 1056 transaction (<code>INSERT pedidos</code> + <code>INSERT outbox</code> + <code>COMMIT</code>) and then the <code>ROLLBACK</code>, which takes both away. <HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>The transaction.</strong> <code>BEGIN; INSERT INTO pedidos …1056…; INSERT INTO outbox …PedidoCriado e-1801…; COMMIT;</code>. Read with PostgreSQL's <code>test_decoding</code>, it came out as <strong>a single transaction</strong>, <code>BEGIN 832 … COMMIT 832</code>, with both rows inside (block below). A second attempt, for a test order, ended in <code>ROLLBACK</code> and <strong>left nothing</strong>: not in the tables, not in the decoded stream.</li>
  <li><strong>The table.</strong> The lab's <code>Outbox_Events</code> has <code>aggregate_type</code>, <code>aggregate_id</code>, <code>event_type</code>, <code>payload</code> (JSON), <code>created_at</code>, <code>processed_at</code>, <code>published_at</code>, <code>error_message</code> and <code>retry_count</code>. The <code>payload</code> is the whole business event.</li>
  <li><strong>The relay.</strong> The lab's <code>outbox_relay.go</code> polls the table every <strong>2 s</strong>, in batches of <strong>100</strong>, with up to <strong>5</strong> attempts per event. It adds <strong>1 s on average</strong> and <strong>2 s in the worst case</strong> to the read model's delay.</li>
  <li><strong>CDC reading the <code>outbox</code> itself.</strong> Debezium's <em>Outbox Event Router</em> expects the columns <code>id</code>, <code>aggregatetype</code>, <code>aggregateid</code>, <code>type</code> and <code>payload</code> and routes by <code>aggregatetype</code>. It removes the <em>poll</em>: latency drops from up to 2 s to <strong>milliseconds</strong>, and the event is still the business one.</li>
  <li><strong>Several relays.</strong> With <code>SELECT … WHERE publicado_em IS NULL ORDER BY id LIMIT 4 FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED</code>, relay A took ids <strong>1, 2, 3 and 4</strong> and B, at the same time, <strong>5, 6, 7 and 8</strong>: nothing in common.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">The cost: the outbox writes more</h3>

<p>The article warns that the outbox can become a bottleneck, and now there is a number. Over 20,000 orders (each with an <code>INSERT</code> and an <code>UPDATE</code> of the status, in a simple table with a primary key; <code>PedidoCriado</code> weighs 326 B in <code>jsonb</code> and <code>PedidoPago</code>, 63 B), the WAL per order was:</p>

<h3 class="sec">If it goes wrong (a): the relay dies between publishing and marking</h3>

<p>The relay publishes the <code>PedidoCriado</code> of 1056 (line 138) and dies <strong>before</strong> marking (line 154). When it returns, the event is still without <code>processed_at</code>, and the relay <strong>publishes it again</strong>. Delivery is <strong>at least once</strong>: the event is never lost, but it can go out twice. The consumer has to be <strong>idempotent</strong> (by <code>evento_id</code>, <code>e-1801</code>); the lab's <code>handler</code> is not (lesson 5).</p>

<h3 class="sec">If it goes wrong (b): two relays, swapped order</h3>

<p>The <code>PedidoCriado</code> of 1056 is id 3 and <code>PedidoPago</code> is id 5. With <code>SKIP LOCKED</code> and two relays, id 3 and id 5 can be published by <strong>different</strong> relays, and 5 can arrive first. Two defenses: the <strong>topic key</strong> should be the <code>pedido_id</code> (same partition, same publishing order) and the consumer checks the <code>versao</code> (module 17, lesson 4). Read in the code: the lab relay's <code>SELECT</code> (lines 99 to 107) does <strong>not</strong> have <code>FOR UPDATE SKIP LOCKED</code>, so two relays would pick the <strong>same</strong> events and publish them twice.</p>

<h3 class="sec">If it goes wrong (c): the table grows</h3>

<p>Each order leaves rows in the <code>outbox</code>, and the relay's <code>UPDATE</code> keeps them there. The lab has a cleanup function (<code>LimparEventosProcessados</code>, which deletes whatever is past its deadline), but nothing guarantees that it runs. The defense is to <strong>delete</strong> or <strong>partition</strong> by day whatever was already published.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>One transaction, two pieces of data.</strong> Order and event go in together or neither goes in, and the <code>ROLLBACK</code> takes both away.</li>
  <li><strong>The event is the business one.</strong> The <code>payload</code> is the whole <code>PedidoCriado</code>, with items and price at the time.</li>
  <li><strong>At-least-once delivery.</strong> Nothing is lost, but it can duplicate: the idempotent consumer is part of the pattern.</li>
  <li><strong>It has a cost.</strong> From 5.3× to 7.6× the WAL per order, 82 KB/s at 30 orders/s with <code>UPDATE</code>.</li>
</WhyItMatters>
