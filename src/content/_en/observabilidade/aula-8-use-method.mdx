<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>The method.</strong> Brendan Gregg described it as a way to <strong>start</strong> a performance investigation: for each resource, three questions, and you go through all the resources before theorizing. The resources are the physical ones (CPU, memory, disk, network) and also the queues and pools.</li>
  <li><strong>Utilization.</strong> How much of the resource is in use, over an interval: CPU at 64%, connections in use ÷ the limit. It is a load and trend indicator; it serves capacity planning.</li>
  <li><strong>Saturation.</strong> The work that <strong>waits</strong> because the resource cannot keep up: the CPU run queue, the requests in the pool queue, <em>iowait</em>, the Kafka consumer's lag. A resource at 99% may still serve, and one with a queue already degrades.</li>
  <li><strong>Errors.</strong> Failure events <strong>of the resource</strong>: an I/O error, an <em>OOMKill</em>, a <em>packet drop</em>, a connection refused by a full pool. In the original method they belong to the <strong>resource</strong>; the service's errors stay in RED (lesson 9).</li>
  <li><strong>The walkthrough.</strong> List the cell's resources, fill in U, S and E for each and look first at what is red in S or E. The method answers "which resource is the bottleneck?" and is silent about the rest.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec"><code>catalogo-3</code> in three lines</h3>

<p>Go back to the module 14 Black Friday, at 20:03. The catalog fleet has 10 replicas, nine at 64% utilization and <code>catalogo-3</code> at <strong>99%</strong>. The fleet, on average, is at (9 × 64 + 99) ÷ 10 = <strong>67.5%</strong>: it looks healthy. By the three USE questions:</p>

<p><strong>Utilization:</strong> 99% on <code>catalogo-3</code>; the fleet average (67.5%) hides it. <strong>Saturation:</strong> the queue. By Little's law (module 14), <code>catalogo-3</code> has 64 req/s × 1.01 s = <strong>64.6</strong> requests in flight, out of a fleet total of 86.4; each healthy replica has 2.4. <strong>Errors:</strong> <strong>1%</strong> of <code>504</code> at 640 req/s. Any of the three, alone, points to the right replica; the average does not.</p>

<h3 class="sec">The 20-connection pool, again</h3>

<p>The module 25 pool is a resource, and USE describes it well. <strong>Utilization:</strong> connections in use ÷ 20. Without the report, 0.4 of 20 (2%). With the 23:00 report, 40.4 requested for 20: <strong>100%</strong>. <strong>Saturation:</strong> whoever waits for a slot, <code>db_pool_waiting</code>. <strong>Errors:</strong> the <code>503</code>s from the full pool, 33% of checkout and 10% of status in the module 25 test. It is the same situation USE sees in other words, with one advantage: the reading <strong>does not depend on knowing what caused it</strong>.</p>

<h3 class="sec">And if it goes wrong</h3>

<p><strong>USE only sees resources.</strong> With <code>2.5.0</code> live, the CPU is at 20%, the pool at 10%, the disk at peace, and <code>pedidos</code> keeps returning shipping 0.00 in Recife. Every resource is <strong>fine</strong>: the defect is in the service. That is the method's limit, and lesson 9 shows what covers the gap.</p>

<p><strong>The fleet average.</strong> <code>catalogo-3</code> at 99% vanishes in a fleet at 67.5%. A <strong>per-fleet</strong> utilization dashboard shows a calm number, while 1 in every 10 requests lives on the saturated replica. USE applies to each <strong>instance</strong>, and then you aggregate.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Saturation shows what utilization hides.</strong> <code>catalogo-3</code> has 64.6 requests in flight and the fleet is at 67.5%.</li>
  <li><strong>The pool is a resource too.</strong> 100% utilization, a queue and 503s: it is module 25 in three lines.</li>
  <li><strong>The method is blind to the service.</strong> With CPU at 20%, <code>2.5.0</code> stays wrong.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={4}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Where the original method is</h4>
  <p>The article cites ACM Queue (<em>Thinking Methodically about Performance</em>), which answers 403 to robots. The method is described by Gregg himself at <code>brendangregg.com/usemethod.html</code>, with checklists per operating system. There, the list of resources includes CPU, memory, network interfaces, disks and buses, and the <em>errors</em> are events of the resource.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the source article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>"Errors" per resource mixes two things.</strong> The article lists both resource errors (<em>I/O errors</em>, <em>OOMKills</em>) and software errors (<em>thread pool exhausted</em>, <em>rate limit exceeded</em>). In the original method the <strong>errors</strong> are events of the resource; the service's stay in RED.</li>
    <li><strong>ACM Queue link.</strong> It answers 403 to robots; the source used here is Gregg's page.</li>
    <li><strong>Math.</strong> The resource table is a reading model. The <code>catalogo-3</code> numbers are from module 14, and the pool's are from module 25 (real k6).</li>
  </ul>

<p class="gancho">All the resources can be fine and the service wrong. USE looks from the inside. What is missing is how to look at the service <strong>from the outside</strong>: how many requests arrive, how many fail and how long they take. That is lesson 9, RED.</p>
