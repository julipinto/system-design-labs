<p class="ponte">In the last lesson Lena waited 1.9 s, the dashboard was green and support had only the code <code>9c1e5a7b</code>. The first signal the team opens is the <strong>metric</strong>, because it is the cheapest and the most aggregated. But why did a percentile dashboard not see that order?</p>

<OneLiner>The <strong>metric</strong> says <strong>that</strong> something changed and <strong>how much</strong>, at low cost. It has three types (counter, <em>gauge</em>, histogram) and its <strong>labels</strong> cost <strong>cardinality</strong>.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Counter.</strong> It only goes up (or goes back to zero when the service restarts). <code>http_server_requests_total</code> counts requests. A counter alone says little; what matters is how much it rises per second, the Prometheus <code>rate()</code> function.</li>
  <li><strong>Gauge.</strong> It goes up and down: <code>db_pool_connections_in_use</code>, <code>replication_lag_seconds</code> (the RPO from module 27). It keeps the <strong>last value</strong>, and nothing else.</li>
  <li><strong>Histogram.</strong> It keeps the distribution: a <strong>cumulative</strong> count per value range, the <em>buckets</em>. It is the right type for response time.</li>
  <li><strong>Percentile.</strong> It comes out of the histogram by <strong>linear interpolation</strong> inside the <em>bucket</em> where the percentile falls. The precision depends on where the <em>buckets</em> are.</li>
  <li><strong>Label.</strong> <code>servico</code>, <code>rota</code>, <code>status</code> and <code>celula</code> split one metric into many <strong>series</strong>. Each combination of values is a stored series, and the count grows fast.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">The 1063 histogram</h3>

<p>Take a window of <strong>1,000 requests</strong> (200 s at 5 per second) of <code>POST /pedidos</code>, measured by <code>http_server_request_duration_seconds</code>. The <em>buckets</em> have limits (<code>le</code>, "less than or equal") at 0.05 · 0.1 · 0.25 · 0.5 · 0.8 · 1 · 2.5 · 5 and +Inf. The 0.8 is there on purpose: it is the SLO limit. The counts are <strong>cumulative</strong>:</p>

<p>The percentile comes from an interpolation. <strong>p50</strong> is request number 500, which is in the 0.25 to 0.5 <em>bucket</em> (from the 400th to the 975th value). Inside it, the values are assumed to be <strong>spread in a straight line</strong>: 0.25 + (500 − 400) ÷ (975 − 400) × 0.25 = <strong>0.293 s</strong>. <strong>p95</strong> (the 950th) falls in the same <em>bucket</em>: 0.25 + 550 ÷ 575 × 0.25 = <strong>0.489 s</strong>. <strong>p99</strong> (the 990th) falls in the 0.5 to 0.8 one: 0.5 + 15 ÷ 17 × 0.3 = <strong>0.765 s</strong>, below the 0.8 s SLO. <strong>p99.9</strong> (the 999th) falls in the 1 to 2.5 one: 1 + 7 ÷ 8 × 1.5 = <strong>2.3125 s</strong>.</p>

<p>What 1063 shows is this rule: <strong>the episode moves neither p95 nor p99</strong>. Eight orders in 1,000 is 0.8%, and p99 discards exactly the 10 slowest. The dashboard stays green and Lena waited 1.9 s. <strong>p99.9</strong> already sees the 8, but gives 2.3125 s for a value that is about 1.9 s: the 1 to 2.5 <em>bucket</em> is wide, and the interpolation only knows the value is somewhere inside. What sets the precision is the <strong>limits of the <em>buckets</em></strong>. Put one near the SLO (0.8) and another near what you fear (2.0).</p>

<h3 class="sec">Percentiles do not add up</h3>

<p>In module 14, each replica sent its p99 to the dashboard, and the dashboard took the average: (4 × 55 + 5 × 74 + 2,100) ÷ 10 = <strong>269 ms</strong>. The true fleet p99, over the 100 requests together, was <strong>2,000 ms</strong>. A percentile <strong>cannot be added or averaged</strong>: Prometheus says quantiles do not aggregate. The right way is to sum the <em>bucket</em> counts from all replicas and compute the percentile <strong>again</strong>. You can look at the same thing from the other side: the average hides the distribution, and <code>catalogo-3</code> at 99% vanished in a fleet at 67.5%.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Technical and business</h3>

<p>The article separates two families. The <strong>technical</strong> ones measure the software: response time, <em>status</em> counters, <em>breaker</em> state. The <strong>business</strong> ones measure what the domain considers success: <code>pedidos_criados_total&#123;celula&#125;</code>, <code>pagamentos_recusados_total&#123;motivo&#125;</code> and the one that found the wrong shipping in lesson 1, <code>pedidos_frete_zero_total&#123;cidade="recife"&#125;</code>. Both sit on the same dashboard. The business ones are the ones that report "available and wrong".</p>

<h3 class="sec">Labels cost series</h3>

<p>Each label splits the metric. In the store: <code>servico</code> (7 values) × <code>rota</code> (12) × <code>status</code> (6) × <code>celula</code> (4) = <strong>2,016 combinations</strong>. A histogram generates <strong>11 series</strong> per combination (8 <code>le</code>, the <code>+Inf</code>, the <code>_sum</code> and the <code>_count</code>): 2,016 × 11 = <strong>22,176 series</strong>. That is fine for a Prometheus. If someone puts <code>cliente_id</code> as a label, it is 22,176 × 1,500,000 = <strong>33.3 billion</strong>. <code>cliente_id</code>, <code>pedido_id</code> and <code>trace_id</code> are <strong>almost unique</strong> values, and almost unique is the opposite of a label: their place is the <strong>log</strong> and the <em>trace</em>.</p>

<h3 class="sec">And if it goes wrong</h3>

<p>There are two ways for a metric to fail without being broken. The first is <strong><code>cliente_id</code> as a label</strong>: 33.3 billion series take down the metrics server, and the dashboard disappears exactly when it is needed most. The second is the <strong>wide <em>bucket</em></strong>: p99.9 gives 2.31 s for a 1.9 s value, and whoever trusts the number looks for the problem in the wrong place. In both cases the metric says <strong>that</strong> something is there and does not let you find <strong>what</strong>.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>The metric is the cheapest.</strong> With 22,176 series, the whole store fits on one dashboard per cell.</li>
  <li><strong>The percentile depends on the <em>buckets</em>.</strong> p99 = 0.765 s, p99.9 = 2.3125 s for a 1.9 s value.</li>
  <li><strong>The episode stays outside p99.</strong> Eight in 1,000 is 0.8%; the metric shows <em>that</em> there is a problem, not <em>which</em> orders.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <p>The interpolation is the one in Prometheus <code>histogram_quantile</code>: it assumes a uniform distribution inside the <em>bucket</em>.
