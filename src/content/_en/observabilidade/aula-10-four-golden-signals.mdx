<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Latency.</strong> The time of a request, measured in <strong>percentiles</strong> and with <strong>two curves</strong>: the one for <strong>successes</strong> and the one for <strong>errors</strong>. A fast error and a slow error are different problems, and mixing the two hides both.</li>
  <li><strong>Traffic.</strong> How much work arrives: orders per second, messages, bytes. It serves to tell <strong>legitimate use</strong> from <strong>amplification</strong>: <em>retries</em>, a mass <em>cache miss</em>, <em>fan-out</em> (the module 21 storm).</li>
  <li><strong>Errors.</strong> The fraction that fails, in the way the customer understands it: <code>5xx</code>, explicit protocol failure and <strong>the <code>200</code> with wrong content</strong>. The <code>4xx</code>s are observed too.</li>
  <li><strong>Saturation.</strong> How close the <strong>tightest</strong> resource is to its limit, and the forecast: not "the disk is at 80%", but "the disk fills in 4 h". The most useful signal for acting <em>before</em>.</li>
  <li><strong>The relation to the others.</strong> The four signals are <strong>RED + saturation</strong>, at the service level. USE remains the lens for the <strong>resource</strong>. A store uses all three, each where it fits.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">Why split the latency</h3>

<p>Suppose the module 21 <em>breaker</em> is open. It refuses the call right away, and <strong>30%</strong> of the requests fail in <strong>2 ms</strong>. The other <strong>70%</strong> answer in <strong>275 ms</strong>, the normal. The average latency is 0.3 × 2 + 0.7 × 275 = <strong>193 ms</strong>: it fell from 275 to 193 ms, <strong>while the system got worse</strong>, because 3 in every 10 customers receive an error. A dashboard with a single average only sees the improvement. With two curves, the success one stays at 275 ms, and the error one shows the fast failure: the problem appears.</p>

<p>The reverse also holds, and the SRE book says so: a <strong>slow</strong> error is worse than a fast error. Whoever receives an error in 2 ms tries again or gives up; whoever waits 8 s for an error has already spent a connection, a thread and their patience. You can only see this difference if the two latencies are separate.</p>

<p>Four cells × four signals are 16 numbers. They answer the article's two questions: "is the system healthy from the user's point of view?" and "which parts are degraded right now?". Each signal feeds a decision: latency and errors give the SLI from lesson 7; traffic, the module 23 capacity plan; saturation, the forecast alert.</p>

<h3 class="sec">And if it goes wrong: monitoring by accumulation</h3>

<p>The article names the opposite of the four signals: <strong>monitoring by accumulation</strong>. The team piles up 300 dashboards and thousands of metrics, and when someone asks "is the store fine?", nobody answers without opening ten of them. What gets lost is the <strong>mental model</strong>. Four signals per cell do not replace the details (the resources and services are still there), but they give a place to start and the basis for the SLO.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Four numbers describe the user.</strong> Latency, traffic, errors and saturation, per cell.</li>
  <li><strong>The average can improve while the system gets worse.</strong> 275 → 193 ms with 30% failing in 2 ms.</li>
  <li><strong>Each lens has its place.</strong> USE for the resource, RED for the service and the four signals for the user.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={5}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">What the SRE book says about latency</h4>
  <p>In the <em>Monitoring Distributed Systems</em> chapter, Google writes that you must <strong>distinguish the latency of successful requests from that of failed ones</strong>: a <code>500</code> caused by losing the database connection, for example, comes out very fast and, if it enters the average, produces a misleading calculation. The same chapter argues for alerting on <strong>symptoms</strong> and using the cause for debugging.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the source article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Latency.</strong> The article says latency "includes both successful and error responses". The SRE book says the <strong>opposite</strong>: you must <strong>distinguish</strong>, because a fast <code>500</code> pulls the average down ("misleading calculations"), and "a <strong>slow</strong> error is even worse than a fast error". In the store: 275 → 193 ms with 30% fast failures.</li>
    <li><strong>References.</strong> The article cites Nanoshots (429 on 10/21/2026) and ACM Queue (403 to robots); neither is used. The Lamport text opens and stays under "Going deeper".</li>
    <li><strong>Math.</strong> The 193.1 ms average (0.3 × 2 + 0.7 × 275) was calculated in Python on 10/21/2026. No real dashboard was built.</li>
  </ul>
