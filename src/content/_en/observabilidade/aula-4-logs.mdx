<p class="ponte">The metric showed that 8 orders took more than 1 s and did not say which, because <code>pedido_id</code> does not fit in a label. The answer to "what happened to <strong>that</strong> order?" lives in the <strong>log</strong>: a timestamped record of something the service did. But each service writes its own, and there are five on Lena's path.</p>

<OneLiner>The log is the <strong>diary of an event</strong>: what happened to <strong>that</strong> order. It is only worth it if it is <strong>structured</strong> and <strong>correlatable</strong> (<code>trace_id</code>, <code>pedido_id</code>, <code>celula</code>, <code>versao</code>), and what limits it is the <strong>cost</strong>.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Six levels.</strong> <strong>TRACE</strong> (every internal step), <strong>DEBUG</strong> (why the system decided that way; the article ties it to crises), <strong>INFO</strong> (the facts of the flow), <strong>WARN</strong> (a deviation the system recovered from), <strong>ERROR</strong> (the operation failed) and <strong>FATAL</strong> (the process cannot go on).</li>
  <li><strong>The actionable WARN.</strong> A good WARN brings context for triage and shows up <strong>before</strong> the incident. The <code>estoque</code> <code>espera por lock</code> is one: it was written before Lena wrote in.</li>
  <li><strong>ERROR is a triage log.</strong> It says what failed, why, where, and how to find the rest of the flow (the <code>trace_id</code>).</li>
  <li><strong>Structure.</strong> JSON with fixed fields (<code>trace_id</code>, <code>pedido_id</code>, <code>celula</code>, <code>servico</code>, <code>versao</code>, <code>level</code>) instead of free text. You can filter and sum by field.</li>
  <li><strong>Index only some.</strong> Indexing the text of every line costs a lot. With fixed fields, you index the <code>trace_id</code> and the <code>level</code>, and the rest stays compressed and cheap, searched only afterwards.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">The five lines of 1063</h3>

<p>Read it in order. The first line is the entry at <code>gw-loja</code>. The third is the one that explains: <code>estoque</code> waited <strong>1,620 ms</strong> for a <em>lock</em> that <code>contagem-estoque</code> (the <code>holder_pid</code> 4412) held on the <code>CANECA-007</code> row. The last one closes it: <code>201</code>, <strong>1,895 ms</strong>. No line alone tells the story, and the five, with the same <code>trace_id</code>, do. Order 1047 from module 6 had 5 lines joined by <code>7f3a…</code>; it is the same mechanism, and that full id is <code>7f3a9c0e5b2d4186a9e3c7d1f0b84e26</code>. (The log fields and messages are kept in Portuguese, as the services write them.)</p>

<p>Two details. The <code>cliente_id</code> may go in the log (it identifies the order), and the customer's <strong>CPF</strong> and <strong>token</strong> may not: personal data and secrets go in masked or do not go in at all. And the <code>trace_id</code> does not replace the timestamp, it only completes it: clocks on different machines diverge by a few milliseconds.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What the diary costs</h3>

<p>The math has few pieces. Each order goes through <strong>5 services</strong>, with ~<strong>3 INFO lines</strong> each, of ~<strong>300 B</strong> in JSON: 5 × 3 × 300 = <strong>4.5 KB per order</strong>. The catalog writes <strong>1 line of 250 B</strong> per request. On a normal day, at 5 orders per second and 40 req/s on the catalog (module 14): 5 × 4.5 = 22.5 KB/s, plus 40 × 250 B = 10 KB/s, or <strong>32.5 KB/s</strong> = <strong>2.81 GB per day</strong>.</p>

<p>Now the Black Friday peak, with <strong>30 orders per second</strong> and <strong>640 req/s</strong>, in a conservative calculation that assumes the peak lasts all day: 135 KB/s + 160 KB/s = 295 KB/s, <strong>25.5 GB per day</strong>, and <strong>765 GB</strong> over 30 days of retention. With <strong>DEBUG</strong> on (the article says it is "used in crises"), about 10 times more: <strong>255 GB per day</strong>.</p>

<h3 class="sec">And if it goes wrong</h3>

<p><strong>DEBUG on during Black Friday.</strong> Someone opens the level to understand a defect and forgets it. That is 255 GB per day, the ingestion cost goes up and search gets slow exactly when you need it. The way out is to open DEBUG per <strong>service and for a limited time</strong>, not across the whole store.</p>

<p><strong>Personal data in the log.</strong> A line with a CPF, email or token stays indexed, copied and retained for 30 days, in a place many people query. Worse: the log is hard to erase. Prevention is masking at the source; the <code>cliente_id</code> is enough to find the order.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>The log answers "why?".</strong> The <code>holder</code> <code>contagem-estoque</code> was written down, one line, before Lena complained.</li>
  <li><strong>The <code>trace_id</code> turns the lines into one story.</strong> Without it, each service writes an island.</li>
  <li><strong>The cost has a number.</strong> 2.81 GB per day on a normal day, 25.5 at the peak and 255 with DEBUG; 765 GB in 30 days.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={4}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Why index only some fields</h4>
  <p>Grafana's Loki stores logs with an index only on the <strong>labels</strong> and leaves the text compressed. Its documentation warns about the same thing as the article: labels with almost-unique values (an id per request) blow up the index. It is the cardinality from lesson 3 again, now for logs: the <code>trace_id</code> is <strong>searched</strong> for, but not used as a label.</p>
