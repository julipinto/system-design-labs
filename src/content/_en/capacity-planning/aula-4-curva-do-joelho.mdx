<p class="ponte">Little gave the target L and left a question: up to what utilization does the <code>pagamentos</code> pod stay within it? The lesson 2 table already showed that response time doesn't grow in a straight line. What's missing is <strong>where</strong> the growth stops being acceptable.</p>

<OneLiner>The response is <strong>nonlinear</strong>: each point of utilization costs more than the last. The <strong>knee</strong> is where the cost of one more point passes a limit the <strong>team chooses</strong>; it is not a corner of the curve.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>The curve.</strong> In M/M/1, W = S ÷ (1 − ρ). There is no point where the curve "breaks": it just gets steeper and steeper.</li>
  <li><strong>The cost of the point.</strong> What <strong>one more point</strong> of utilization costs on <code>pagamentos</code> (S = 50 ms): at <strong>50%</strong>, 2 ms; at <strong>70%</strong>, 5.6 ms; at <strong>80%</strong>, 12.5 ms; at <strong>90%</strong>, 50 ms; at <strong>95%</strong>, 200 ms; at <strong>99%</strong>, 5,000 ms.</li>
  <li><strong>Choose the limit.</strong> The team decides how much it is willing to pay per point. The store chose zones, and the knee is the region where the cost becomes unacceptable.</li>
  <li><strong>Operate behind the knee.</strong> The operating target sits before it, leaving margin for variability and bursts.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<p>This reconciles the article (knee at 80–85%) with module 14 (70%): <strong>the knee is where the response leaves the acceptable range; 70% is the operating target that leaves margin for variability</strong>. Both fit on the same curve.</p>

<h3 class="sec">The knee in other metrics</h3>

<p>CPU is not the only metric with a knee. <strong>Memory</strong>, the <strong>connection pool</strong> (module 11), the <strong>Kafka queue</strong> (the lag) and <strong>concurrency</strong> (L) each have a curve, and each one's knee is <strong>its own</strong>. Whatever falls first rules: a <code>pagamentos</code> at 40% CPU can have its pool at the knee (lesson 3).</p>

<h3 class="sec">If it goes wrong: "no errors yet, let's run at 95%"</h3>

<p>At 95%, <code>pagamentos</code> W is <strong>1 s</strong>, twenty times the service time, and each extra point costs <strong>200 ms</strong>. Any burst, like the 20:00 newsletter from module 13, throws the p95 to about 3 s (the exponential p95 at 95% is 2,996 ms). <code>lb-loja</code>'s 2 s timeout turns into a <strong>504</strong> for the customer. With no errors the day before, the store finds the knee in the worst minute.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Each point costs more than the last.</strong> At 70% it costs 5.6 ms; at 90% it already costs 9 times more.</li>
  <li><strong>The knee is a choice.</strong> It depends on what the business accepts to pay in milliseconds.</li>
  <li><strong>Each metric has its own.</strong> The plan has to look at CPU, pool, queue and concurrency together.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={3}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Correction to the base article and what the curve says</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>"Latency grows linearly."</strong> The article writes that, in normal terms, latency grows linearly up to saturation. In a queue the growth is already <strong>convex from the start</strong> (W = S ÷ (1 − ρ)); it only <em>looks</em> linear up to about 50%. The knee is <strong>a choice of limit</strong> on a smooth curve: in M/M/1 there is no corner.</li>
    <li><strong>The saturation margins.</strong> The article suggests 80–85% as the limit. The store's plan uses 70% as the operating target, and the 80–85% knee as the alert point.</li>
  </ul>
