<p class="ponte">By the end of module 22 the store knew how to <strong>ship</strong> without taking itself down: rolling, blue/green, canary, shadow, flags and rings. But every spring the sales team asks a question no deploy answers: <strong>"will next year's Black Friday hold up?"</strong> Nobody has done the math. The 2026 one was measured in module 14; the 2027 one (Friday, <strong>11/26/2027</strong>) is still an unknown.</p>

<OneLiner><strong>Capacity planning</strong> does not predict the future. It <strong>turns what was measured into limits and a plan</strong>, before the incident: how much each service needs, in which order the links break, how much it costs and what is still unknown.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>The inherited numbers.</strong> Black Friday 2026 left a table, measured at <strong>20:03</strong> (module 14). It is the starting point and <strong>does not change</strong> in this module.</li>
  <li><strong>The question becomes scenarios.</strong> Sales does not give a number, it gives an intention. The planner answers with three hypotheses per service, from the most likely to the most ambitious.</li>
  <li><strong>Each scenario becomes load.</strong> Lessons 2 to 6 teach how to measure the load and spread it over time; lesson 7 projects it to 2027.</li>
  <li><strong>The load meets the limits.</strong> Lesson 8 measures what the whole flow can take and lesson 9 closes with the <strong>limits sheet</strong>, the cost and the re-evaluation triggers.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">The three answers for 2027</h3>

<p>With that table in hand, the planner does not answer "it holds" or "it doesn't". It answers with one <strong>scenario</strong> per service, and each scenario has an owner: the history, the business event or the worst case.</p>

<div class="sr-tablewrap">
<table class="sr-table">
  <thead><tr><th></th><th>Catalog (req/s)</th><th>Checkout (orders/s)</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><th>Trend</th><td><strong>750</strong> (the series' straight line)</td><td><strong>45</strong> (30 × 950 ÷ 640)</td></tr>
    <tr><th>With the event / the goal</th><td><strong>950</strong> (the new home screen, +15%)</td><td><strong>200</strong> (the sales goal)</td></tr>
    <tr><th>Stress</th><td><strong>1,140</strong> (950 × 1.2)</td><td><strong>300</strong> (1.5 × the goal)</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
</div>

<p>The difference between <strong>45</strong> and <strong>200</strong> orders per second is <strong>155</strong>, and the customer trend does not explain it. It only exists if <strong>partners through the API</strong> (<code>mkt-01</code> and 3 others, module 8) sell without browsing the catalog; that is why the catalog stays at 950. The planner asks the business this question instead of guessing the answer.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Reactive × preventive</h3>

<p>Without a plan, capacity is discovered during the incident: the fleet fills up, p95 climbs, someone adds replicas in a hurry. With a plan, the same number shows up in October, in a document, with a cost. The work is the same; what changes is <strong>when</strong> you pay for it.</p>

<p>The route the rest of the module follows has <strong>8 steps</strong>, and lesson 9 applies all of them to the store:</p>

<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Delimit the flow</strong> to be sized.</li>
  <li><strong>Estimate the load</strong> (TPS, concurrency, payload).</li>
  <li><strong>Measure each component's individual throughput</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>Derive the systemic one</strong>: the minimum of the chain.</li>
  <li><strong>Project the growth</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>Evaluate the cost</strong> and the margins.</li>
  <li><strong>Define the operating limits</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>Test</strong> with load (module 24).</li>
</ol>

<h3 class="sec">If it goes wrong: sizing for what already happened</h3>

<p>The most common mistake is planning with the number already seen: <strong>640 req/s</strong> and 10 replicas, "which was enough in 2026". In 2027 the new-screen event arrives and demand goes to <strong>950</strong>. The 10 replicas reach <strong>95%</strong> usage, and the module 14 formula, R = 10 ms + 10 ms ÷ (1 − ρ), gives <strong>210 ms</strong> on average. The p95 goes back to the 800 ms of module 14, and the problem shows up at 20:03, the minute of the newsletter, with sales watching.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>The plan is a model.</strong> It is useful because it gives a number to discuss before the incident, not because it gets the future right.</li>
  <li><strong>Scenarios, not guessing.</strong> Trend, goal and stress make clear who decides what: engineering calculates the cost, the business picks the goal.</li>
  <li><strong>The question to the business is part of it.</strong> The extra 155 orders per second come from no chart, they come from the partner API.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <h4 class="deep-h">What the base article leaves out</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>The cost of the goal.</strong> The gap between the trend and the sales goal is a business decision, priced in vCPU. Planning only by the trend hides the bill.</li>
    <li><strong>The re-evaluation trigger.</strong> A plan without the rule "if demand passes X, redo the math" ages in silence (lesson 9).</li>
    <li><strong>The freeze and the pre-scale.</strong> The plan includes stopping changes before the event and raising replicas before the newsletter (lesson 9).</li>
    <li><strong>Nominal × operational capacity.</strong> Module 24 measures the second; this module calculates the first.</li>
  </ul>
