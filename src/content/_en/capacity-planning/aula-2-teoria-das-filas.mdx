<p class="ponte">Last lesson the plan was left with a question underneath: why is 70% usage healthy and 95% not? The answer lies in something <code>pagamentos</code> has and module 14 already used without naming: a <strong>queue</strong>. Each pod serves one order at a time, and the ones that arrive while it works wait.</p>

<OneLiner>Every queue has an <strong>arrival (λ)</strong>, a <strong>service (μ)</strong> and an <strong>order</strong>. The waiting time depends on the <strong>utilization (ρ = λ ÷ μ)</strong> and on the <strong>variability</strong> of the service, not just on the average.</OneLiner>

</DoIt>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>The two numbers.</strong> A <code>pagamentos</code> pod spends <strong>50 ms</strong> per order (S = 50 ms), so it serves <strong>μ = 1 ÷ 0.05 = 20 orders/s</strong>. The arrival rate is λ. The utilization is <strong>ρ = λ ÷ μ</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>The wait grows with ρ.</strong> In the simplest model (M/M/1: random arrivals and services, one server), the response time is <strong>W = S ÷ (1 − ρ)</strong>. At 50% it is double S; at 90%, ten times; at 99%, a hundred.</li>
  <li><strong>Variability pushes the curve.</strong> Two services with the same average rate can have very different curves: the more irregular the service time, the more waiting. The Pollaczek–Khinchine formula measures this with the <strong>CV</strong> (coefficient of variation).</li>
  <li><strong>Every hop is a queue.</strong> The connection pool, the lock, the database connection and the retry also form queues. <code>pagamentos</code> has a CPU queue and a pool queue.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<p>The p95 uses the exponential distribution: <strong>W × ln 20</strong>. Between 50% and 95% the average goes from 100 ms to 1 s without the pod accepting a single order per second more than it can serve. What grows is the wait.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Variability: same average, another experience</h3>

<p>M/M/1 assumes the service time varies like an exponential (CV = 1). In practice <code>pagamentos</code> mixes light and heavy orders. The <strong>Pollaczek–Khinchine</strong> formula (M/G/1) is <strong>W = S + ρ ÷ (1 − ρ) × (1 + CV²) ÷ 2 × S</strong>. At 80%, with S = 50 ms:</p>

<p>Same capacity and same utilization, with an average time <strong>3.7 times longer</strong> (550 ÷ 150). A dashboard that only looks at "CPU at 80%" can't see the difference.</p>

<h3 class="sec">M/M/1 is pessimistic for the store</h3>

<p><code>lb-loja</code> does <strong>round robin</strong> (module 7), which makes each pod's arrivals more regular than a random draw. In module 24's k6, 30 orders/s (2 <code>pagamentos</code> pods at 75%) gave an average of <strong>157 ms across the whole chain</strong>, below the roughly 259 ms M/M/1 suggests. Planning with M/M/1 leaves slack; only measuring says how much.</p>

<p><strong>Autoscaling</strong> doesn't fix variability either. The HPA (module 14) reacts in about 60 s and raises the <strong>number of servers</strong>, not the regularity; the short-term burst is what the queue feels before it.</p>

<h3 class="sec">If it goes wrong: the dashboard says 80% and the queue says 550 ms</h3>

<p><code>pagamentos</code> at 80% with CV = 2 answers in <strong>550 ms</strong>, 3.7 times that of CV = 0. The service owner looks at "CPU 80%" and the utilization seems healthy; nothing there explains why the p95 is so high. The cause is the variability of the orders (light and heavy mixed), and the fix is to separate the two types, not to add replicas.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>The waiting time depends on ρ and on CV.</strong> Equal utilization does not mean equal experience.</li>
  <li><strong>Every hop is a queue.</strong> Pool, lock, connection and retry behave the same way: anything above ~80% makes the wait jump.</li>
  <li><strong>M/M/1 is a bound, not a forecast.</strong> It serves to plan with slack and to know what to measure in module 24.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={4}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the base article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>"Variability instead of percentiles."</strong> The article presents CV and standard deviation as metrics that replace percentiles. In a queue, variability is the <strong>factor</strong> (Pollaczek–Khinchine) and percentiles remain what the customer feels. The two add up: average, CV and p99.</li>
    <li><strong>M/M/1 is conservative.</strong> The balancer's round robin and services with CV &lt; 1 make the real queue shorter. The article lacks <strong>M/G/1</strong> (Pollaczek–Khinchine) and <strong>M/M/c</strong> (Erlang C): one shared pool is more efficient than several small ones (module 25).</li>
    <li><strong>Autoscaling does not "raise μ temporarily".</strong> It raises the <strong>capacity</strong> (the number of servers) with a delay of about 60 s (module 14). Short-term variability does not go through it. The article also lacks the option to scale by <strong>concurrency (L)</strong> or by queue, and not only by CPU.</li>
  </ul>
