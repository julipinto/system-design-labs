<p class="ponte">Last lesson the pool of 20 connections was split into 16 + 4 and the customer stopped getting <code>503</code>, but the split applied to only <strong>one</strong> thing, the connections. A <code>pedidos</code> pod has other finite resources: threads, queues, memory, the node it runs on. Splitting everything equally makes no sense. What is missing is <strong>what</strong> to split, and how strongly.</p>

<OneLiner>The <strong>logical</strong> bulkhead splits <strong>execution</strong> resources (threads, queues, connections, limits): it is cheap. The <strong>physical</strong> one splits <strong>infrastructure</strong> (nodes, clusters, zones, regions): it is expensive and stronger.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Thread pool.</strong> Hystrix gives a thread pool <strong>per dependency</strong>: if the call hangs, only the threads in that pool hang. Resilience4j has the <code>FixedThreadPoolBulkhead</code>, with a fixed pool and a bounded queue. The cost is context switching and the queue.</li>
  <li><strong>Semaphore.</strong> It counts <strong>concurrent</strong> calls and refuses the one that goes over the limit (<code>maxConcurrentCalls</code> in Resilience4j's <code>SemaphoreBulkhead</code>). It runs on the caller's thread, with no queue, and the <em>timeout</em> stays with the client. With asynchronous I/O the semaphore is enough. With a legacy library that <strong>blocks</strong>, only a dedicated pool prevents the hang.</li>
  <li><strong>Connections, queues and limits.</strong> PgBouncer limits connections per database and user (<code>default_pool_size</code>). PostgreSQL has <code>max_connections</code> of <strong>100</strong>, with 3 reserved for the superuser. Envoy limits connections and pending requests per destination (1,024 by default, in <em>circuit breaking</em>), and Istio exposes this in the <code>DestinationRule</code>'s <code>ConnectionPoolSettings</code>. Queues and topics per domain belong here too.</li>
  <li><strong>Node pools.</strong> In Kubernetes, <em>taints</em> and <em>tolerations</em> reserve nodes for whoever tolerates them. <code>checkout</code> runs on one node pool and <code>batch</code> on another. This is not a library setting: it is a <strong>separate machine</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>Clusters, zones and regions.</strong> Domains with incompatible goals or high criticality get their own cluster. The isolation becomes structural, and the cost goes up with it.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">How much to give each compartment</h3>

<p>Isolating is not enough: the size of each compartment needs a calculation. The rule is <strong>connections = λ × W × headroom</strong>. For interactive traffic (checkout plus status), 0.2 + 0.2 = <strong>0.4</strong> connections in use. With <strong>10×</strong> headroom for variability (module 23), <strong>4</strong> is enough. For batch, the report <strong>wants</strong> 10 × 4 = 40, but the database can only take a few heavy queries at the same time, so it gets a cap of <strong>4</strong>.</p>

<p>By Little, the split pool would be <strong>4 + 4 = 8</strong>. The other <strong>12</strong> connections <strong>go back</strong> to the database's <code>max_connections</code> (100, 97 for non-superusers, modules 11 and 15). <strong>Splitting can save connections.</strong> The lesson 1 k6 used 16 + 4 = 20 only to compare with the single pool at the <strong>same total</strong>; interactive has 40× headroom there, and the cost is idle connections.</p>

<p>And the report? Instead of squeezing it on the primary, the better fix is <strong>physical</strong>: it reads from the <strong>Rio replica</strong> (module 17). The 300 ms lag on Black Friday does not matter for a report, and the heavy query <strong>does not touch the primary</strong>: 0 connections and 0% of the <code>shard</code>'s write capacity.</p>

<h3 class="sec">The split has a price: statistical multiplexing</h3>

<p>A big pool makes better use of its workers: when one becomes free, any flow can use it. This is <strong>statistical multiplexing</strong>. When you split, each compartment faces its own bursts alone. The math is <strong>Erlang C</strong> (the M/M/c queue from module 23). With <strong>12 workers in 1 pool</strong>, 70% load (8.4 erlangs) and service time S = 50 ms, the probability of waiting is <strong>18.4%</strong> and the average wait is <strong>2.6 ms</strong>. The <strong>same 12 in 3 pools of 4</strong>: <strong>42.9%</strong> chance of waiting and <strong>17.9 ms</strong>. At 80% load: 36.9% and 7.7 ms against 59.6% and 37.3 ms.</p>

<p>To <strong>match</strong> the probability of waiting (≈ 18.4%), each pool needs <strong>5</strong> workers: 15 in total, <strong>+25%</strong>. To match the average wait, <strong>6</strong> each, 18 in total, <strong>+50%</strong>. Splitting a pool <strong>worsens the normal case</strong> and <strong>improves the worst case</strong>. That is the trade the bulkhead makes, and that is why compartment size comes from a calculation and not a guess.</p>

<h3 class="sec">And if it goes wrong: logical on a shared node</h3>

<p>Picture <code>relatorios</code> with no memory limits, running on the same <strong>node</strong> as 3 <code>pedidos</code> pods. The <code>pedidos</code> thread pool is intact, but the <strong>node's memory</strong> is not a pool. The leak pressures the node, the kubelet evicts pods (<em>node-pressure eviction</em>) and the OOM killer hits the neighbors. The article calls this <em>shared fate</em>. The logical compartment did not hold, because the resource that ran out was <strong>underneath</strong> it.</p>

<p>The fix is physical, and it completes the logical one: <code>requests</code> and <code>limits</code> on each pod (which set the QoS class, deciding who is evicted first) and <strong>one node pool for <code>checkout</code> and another for <code>batch</code></strong>, with <em>taints</em> and <em>tolerations</em>. This scenario is qualitative: the figure describes the mechanism, and the memory leak was not run on Kubernetes.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Logical is cheap and physical is strong.</strong> A semaphore costs nothing; a node pool costs machines. The more critical the flow, the further down the list the isolation should go.</li>
  <li><strong>Size comes from a calculation.</strong> Little gives 4 + 4 and returns 12 connections to the database; Erlang C shows what you pay in waiting for splitting.</li>
  <li><strong>A logical bulkhead alone has a hole.</strong> If the resource that runs out is the node's memory, no thread pool helps.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the source article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>"If two flows share the same resources, they have no bulkheads".</strong> True for <strong>total</strong> isolation. There is <strong>reservation with borrowing</strong> (a guaranteed part and a shared part), which lowers the cost. The price of a rigid split is losing statistical multiplexing (Erlang C: from 18.4% to 42.9%; +25% workers make up for it).</li>
    <li><strong>"Thread pool" as the only logical example.</strong> The article does not tell <strong>semaphore</strong> (counts concurrent calls, no queue) apart from <strong>thread pool</strong> (isolates blocking, with a queue and context switching). Hystrix and Resilience4j document both.</li>
    <li><strong>Sizing is missing.</strong> The article says "partition" and does not say how much. Here the size comes from Little (4 + 4) and the cost from Erlang C.</li>
    <li><strong>What was not run:</strong> a real Hystrix or Resilience4j and Kubernetes with node pools. The calculations (Little and Erlang C) were redone in Python.</li>
  </ul>
