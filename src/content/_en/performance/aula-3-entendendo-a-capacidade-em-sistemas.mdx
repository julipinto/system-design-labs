<p class="ponte">In the last lesson the store found <code>catalogo-3</code> by looking per replica: the fleet's average CPU, 67.5%, looked calm, and it was at 99%. That leaves a bigger question, how much the catalog can take before the response goes past the goal, and another on the checkout side: what happens when demand goes past what a service can handle.</p>

<OneLiner><strong>Capacity</strong> is the most work the system serves with an acceptable response, and the <strong>bottleneck</strong> is the link where demand goes past capacity: the catalog handles <strong>1,000 req/s</strong> but is only safe up to <strong>700</strong>, and the whole checkout is worth its weakest link, <code>pagamentos</code>, at <strong>40 orders/s</strong>.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>Start with the math for one replica. Each request to the product page uses <strong>10 ms of CPU</strong> (and 10 ms waiting for cache and DB, without occupying the CPU, which module 11 already showed). So 1 s ÷ 10 ms = <strong>100 req/s per replica</strong>, and the 10 replicas give <strong>1,000 req/s</strong>. The "100 requests per second" in module 8 were for <strong>one</strong> replica.</li>
  <li>But capacity is not "the most before it breaks". Near the limit, requests start waiting for each other, and response time rises as a curve with a <strong>knee</strong>. In the module's toy queue model, R(ρ) = 10 ms + 10 ms ÷ (1 − ρ), where ρ is CPU utilization: <strong>43 ms at 70%</strong>, 60 ms at 80%, 110 ms at 90%, 210 ms at 95% and <strong>1,010 ms at 99%</strong>. That 99% utilization is the Black Friday "2 s", in order of magnitude. That is why the catalog's safe limit is <strong>70%</strong>, 700 req/s.</li>
  <li>Little's law gives the size of the queue: <strong>L = λ × W</strong>, requests in flight = arrivals per second × time each one takes. At 640 req/s and 0.135 s on average, that is <strong>86.4 requests in flight</strong> across the fleet. <code>catalogo-3</code> alone has 64 req/s × 1.01 s = <strong>64.6</strong>, three quarters of everything, against 2.4 on each healthy replica.</li>
  <li>Now checkout, in <strong>orders per second</strong>. The order goes through <code>pedidos</code> (3 × 1 vCPU, 33 ms of CPU, capacity <strong>90/s</strong>), through <code>pagamentos</code> (2 × 1 vCPU, 50 ms, <strong>40/s</strong>), through <code>estoque</code> (2 × 1 vCPU, 2 ms, <strong>1,000/s</strong>) and is written to the <code>pedidos</code> DB, a PostgreSQL primary at <strong>150/s</strong>. The <strong>bottleneck</strong> is demand bigger than a link's capacity.</li>
  <li>Whoever does not see the bottleneck only looks at the total. Today it is <strong>30 orders/s</strong>: <code>pedidos</code> at 33%, <code>pagamentos</code> at <strong>75%</strong> (response of 50 ms ÷ 0.25 = 200 ms, four times the time without a queue) and <code>estoque</code> at 3%. The weakest link already shows, and the math on the total hides it.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">The bottleneck is the link, and it moves</h3>

<p>A <strong>doubled Black Friday</strong> would be 60 orders/s. <code>pedidos</code> gets through (90), but <code>pagamentos</code> only handles <strong>40</strong>: 40 per second go out and <strong>20 per second back up</strong>, 33% degradation. In 60 s that is 1,200 orders waiting, and <code>estoque</code> sits at 4% its whole life. It is the article's math applied to the store: it uses three services of 100, 60 and 300 per second and an input of 120, and the output is 60, 50% of the input, whatever the capacity of the strongest link.</p>

<p>The excess does not vanish: it either <strong>becomes a queue</strong> (and the queue raises response time, by Little's law) or <strong>is dropped</strong> (an error). And there is a second-order effect: fixing one bottleneck sends more load to the next link, which can become the next one. Scaling <code>pagamentos</code> from 2 to 4 replicas takes the chain to min(90, 80, 150) = <strong>80/s</strong>; at 100 orders/s the next link is <code>pedidos</code> (90/s). The bottleneck moves.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Cost per transaction</h3>

<p>Capacity costs too. With 1 replica = 1 vCPU, a fixed fleet of 10 replicas for 24 h is <strong>240 replica-hours</strong>. An elastic fleet, with 10 replicas only for the 4 h of the peak and 2 the rest of the time, spends 10 × 4 + 2 × 20 = <strong>80 replica-hours</strong>, 33% of the fixed one, three times less. The <strong>cost per transaction</strong> is the total cost ÷ the number of transactions (per 1,000 orders, × 1,000): it falls when utilization rises and rises when the fleet sits idle.</p>

<h3 class="sec">If it goes wrong: scaling the wrong link</h3>

<p>Someone looks at the dashboard, sees "checkout slow" and doubles <code>estoque</code> from 2 to 4 replicas (2,000 orders/s of capacity). Nothing changes: the output stays at <strong>40 orders/s</strong>, the 20 per second keep backing up, and the store's CPU bill doubled for a service that was at 4%. The capacity that matters is the weakest link's, and only measuring per link shows which one that is.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Capacity has a unit.</strong> 100 req/s per replica and 1,000 req/s for the catalog fleet; 40 orders/s for checkout.</li>
  <li><strong>The safe limit sits before 100%.</strong> At 70% the response is 43 ms; at 99% it is 1 s.</li>
  <li><strong>The weakest link rules.</strong> Checkout's output is worth 40/s, no matter how much <code>estoque</code> can take.</li>
  <li><strong>Scaling costs.</strong> 240 against 80 replica-hours for the same day, and the cost per transaction shows the idleness.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the base article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Backpressure is something else.</strong> The article uses the fluid-physics definition and gives an example that is about <strong>bottleneck and backing up</strong> (service B backs up 40 per second). In software, "backpressure" usually means the <strong>mechanism</strong> by which the consumer tells the producer to slow down: reactive streams, TCP flow control, <code>429</code> or <code>503</code> with <code>Retry-After</code>. This module calls the example a bottleneck in the chain and leaves the mechanism for module 21, Resilience Patterns. What the article does not say is where the excess goes: it becomes a queue or is dropped.</li>
    <li><strong>Capacity per replica depends on the mix.</strong> The article treats the 10 or 15 TPS per replica as a constant. They change with the <strong>type</strong> of request (the 20 ms page against the 900 ms search): measure per type, with a load test (module 24).</li>
  </ul>
