<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>Google's SRE book proposes four signals: <strong>response time</strong> (its "latency"), <strong>traffic</strong>, <strong>errors</strong> and <strong>saturation</strong>. In the store at 20:03, the response time is the one from lesson 1, traffic is 640 req/s, errors are 1% and saturation, we will see, is the one with a queue.</li>
  <li>Three of them come from simple math. <strong>Utilization</strong> is used ÷ available × 100: 1,024 of 2,048 MB is 50%. <strong>Throughput</strong> is work ÷ time: 6,000 requests in 60 s are 100 req/s, and in the store 38,400 in 60 s are <strong>640 req/s</strong>. <strong>Error rate</strong> is errors ÷ total × 100: 1 in 100 is <strong>1%</strong>, ten times above the 0.1% goal.</li>
  <li>Response time does not fit in a single number. Take a <strong>sample</strong> of 100 requests from the 20:03 minute, drawn from the 38,400, and sort it. The <strong>percentile</strong> p is the value below which p% of the responses fall; with the nearest-rank method, it is the value at position ⌈p% × n⌉. With n = 100: <strong>average 135 ms, p50 32, p90 74, p95 800, p99 2,000, maximum 2,100</strong>.</li>
  <li>The 100 requests come from two worlds. Nine replicas at 64% answer in 26 to 74 ms (average 37.8). <code>catalogo-3</code>, at 99%, answers in 150 to 2,100 ms (average 1,010): the heavy 900 ms searches from module 7 landed almost all on it, because round robin counts requests, not effort. Mix the two and the average comes out at 135, far from both.</li>
  <li><strong>Saturation</strong> is the work that <strong>waits</strong>: the request queue, the full connection pool, the load average above the number of cores. <code>catalogo-3</code> has a queue and the others do not. It only shows up when you look <strong>per replica</strong>, and the whole fleet, at 67.5% CPU on average, looks healthy.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">If it goes wrong: the average of the p99s</h3>

<p>Each replica reports <strong>its own</strong> p99 to the dashboard. With 10 requests per replica, each one's p99 is its maximum: 4 replicas at 55 ms, 5 at 74 ms and <code>catalogo-3</code> at 2,100 ms. The dashboard joins everything with an average, (4 × 55 + 5 × 74 + 2,100) ÷ 10 = <strong>269 ms</strong>, and shows a p99 that looks acceptable. The true fleet p99, computed over the 100 requests together, is <strong>2,000 ms</strong>.</p>

<p>You do not add percentiles or average them. The right way is to merge the <strong>histograms</strong> (the counts per time range, the buckets) and compute the percentile again over the whole set. And there is a second caution: with 100 samples, the p99 is just the 99th value, and a single slow request moves it. It only settles with a thousand samples or more.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Each signal answers a question.</strong> Is it slow? Is a lot arriving? Is it failing? Is it full?</li>
  <li><strong>The p95 is red while the average is green.</strong> The 300 ms goal looks at the p95: 800 ms blows it, and a 135 ms average does not warn you.</li>
  <li><strong>The per-replica view finds the hot one.</strong> The fleet at 67.5% hides <code>catalogo-3</code> at 99%.</li>
  <li><strong>Aggregating percentiles misleads.</strong> 269 ms on the dashboard against 2,000 ms for real.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the base article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Utilization is not saturation.</strong> The article calls a resource near 100% usage "saturated". By Brendan Gregg's USE method (utilization, saturation and errors) and by the SRE book, saturation is <strong>work waiting</strong>: a queue, a full connection pool, load average above the cores. A resource at 100% with nobody waiting is not saturated, and the response can get worse before 100% (the knee in the next lesson).</li>
    <li><strong>Percentiles are not aggregated.</strong> The article explains what p95 and p99 are, but not that you do not average them, nor that p99 with 100 samples is just the 99th value. The Prometheus documentation shows the way with histograms.</li>
  </ul>
