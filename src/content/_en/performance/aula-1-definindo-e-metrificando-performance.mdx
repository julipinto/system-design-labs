<p class="ponte">Module 13 ended with the store talking in every possible way and with one Black Friday itch: the catalog answered in <strong>2 s</strong> for some customers, and nobody could say how much the store can take, where the bottleneck is, or whether turning on more machines would have been enough. The 20:00 newsletter (50,000 emails at 100 per second, 500 s) made the clicks hit the catalog between 20:01 and 20:09. "The store is slow" is not a problem anyone can solve, and this is the first module of numbers: what to measure, where to measure it and against which goal.</p>

<OneLiner>Performance is the time that <strong>whoever uses it feels</strong>, and it only becomes engineering when it has a <strong>number, a measuring point and a goal</strong>: response time is network plus processing, measured at the client, at the load balancer and at the replica, and compared with a goal such as "p95 under 300 ms".</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>It is 20:03 on Black Friday. The catalog has <strong>10 replicas of 1 vCPU</strong> (<code>catalogo-1</code> to <code>catalogo-10</code>) behind <code>lb-loja</code>, with unweighted round robin, and the traffic is the product page, <code>{"GET /produtos/{id}"}</code>. "Slow" breaks down into three questions: <strong>what</strong> is slow (which request), <strong>where</strong> (at which point of the trip) and <strong>how much</strong> (which number, against which goal).</li>
  <li>For the "how much", the first number is <strong>response time</strong>: what passes between the client asking and receiving. It is <strong>network latency</strong> plus <strong>processing time</strong>. On a normal day, Lena, in Recife, pays <strong>80 ms</strong> of round trip to the store (the example RTT) and the server spends <strong>20 ms</strong> (10 ms of CPU and 10 ms waiting for the cache and the document DB). Her browser reads <strong>100 ms</strong>; the server dashboard, 20 ms.</li>
  <li>For the "where", you measure at <strong>three points</strong>: at the client, at <code>lb-loja</code> and at the replica. At 20:03:07 the page for Lena's cat takes <strong>2.08 s</strong> in the browser, <strong>2,000 ms</strong> at the load balancer and <strong>2,000 ms</strong> at the replica. The 80 ms difference is the network, which did not change; the degradation happened inside <code>catalogo-3</code>.</li>
  <li>To know whether 2 s is a problem, you need the <strong>goal</strong>. In the module's example: <strong>p95 under 300 ms</strong> and <strong>errors under 0.1%</strong>, measured at <code>lb-loja</code>, which cuts the request at <strong>2 s</strong> (timeout). Goals come from non-functional requirements: a real-time system and a long-term archive expect different things.</li>
  <li>And the number depends on how much traffic arrives. An ordinary day has <strong>40 req/s</strong>, a replica at 40% CPU and a 26.7 ms response. The Black Friday average is <strong>400 req/s</strong> (the 10 replicas, the 10× from module 6) and the 20:03 peak reaches <strong>640 req/s</strong>, 64 per replica.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">If it goes wrong: the dashboard that only shows the average</h3>

<p>The store dashboard draws a single line, the fleet's <strong>average</strong> response time, and at 20:03 it reads <strong>135 ms</strong>. The goal is 300 ms, so the line is green. Meanwhile, Lena waited 2 s for the cat page, and the black t-shirt page for <strong>Davi</strong> (20:03:12) took 2.1 s: it went past the timeout, he got a <code>504</code>, reloaded, and the second attempt took 61 ms at the load balancer.</p>

<p>At 20:03:30 Davi closes <strong>order 1052</strong>: 3 black t-shirts, 147.00, plus 9.00 shipping to SP, 156.00 total. Davi insisted after the <code>504</code> and bought. Whoever gave up at the first error shows up in no average, and looking only at the green dashboard nobody would know they exist.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>"Slow" becomes a decision.</strong> With a number, a measuring point and a goal, you can say where to act and when to stop.</li>
  <li><strong>Three points say where it hurts.</strong> The difference between the browser, the load balancer and the replica separates the network from the server.</li>
  <li><strong>The goal decides what is a problem.</strong> 2 s for Lena is a defect, and for a nightly report it would be normal.</li>
  <li><strong>The average lies.</strong> A green 135 ms and 2 s on the customer's screen coexist in the same minute.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <h4 class="deep-h">Correction to the base article: latency and response time</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Two names for different things.</strong> The article calls the <strong>network</strong> time latency and the sum with processing response time. Google's SRE book uses "latency" for the time to <strong>serve</strong> the request, and separates the ones that succeeded from the ones that failed: a fast error cannot go into the average. The module uses <strong>response time</strong> for what the client sees and warns when a dashboard calls that "latency".</li>
  </ul>
