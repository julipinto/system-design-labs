<p class="ponte">In the last lesson <code>pagamentos</code> stayed at 40 orders per second against a demand of 60, and the store found out that the weakest link's capacity rules checkout. Now the question is how to increase that capacity. There are two paths: swap the machine for a bigger one or put more identical machines together.</p>

<OneLiner><strong>Scaling</strong> is adding or removing capacity: <strong>vertical</strong> changes the size of one unit (1 to 8 vCPU on the same replica, which gives 600 req/s), <strong>horizontal</strong> changes the number of units (10 replicas of 1 vCPU, which give 1,000 req/s).</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>A bus company with queues at the stops has both exits. Swapping the buses for double-deckers is <strong>scaling vertically</strong>; buying more identical buses is <strong>scaling horizontally</strong>. In the store, vertical is <code>catalogo</code> going from 1 vCPU to <strong>8 vCPU</strong> on a single replica (<strong>scale up</strong>, and <strong>scale down</strong> to go back). Horizontal is turning on more replicas (<strong>scale out</strong>, and <strong>scale in</strong> to remove them).</li>
  <li>Vertical does not yield 8 times. If the part that does not parallelize is the one from <code>relatorios</code> in module 11, <strong>1/21</strong> of the work (4.76%), Amdahl's law gives 1 ÷ (1/21 + (20/21) ÷ 8) = <strong>6.0 times</strong>: <strong>600 req/s</strong>, not 800. That is 75 req/s per vCPU against 100 on horizontal. It requires restarting the pod (Kubernetes has in-place resizing, see the sources) and it has a ceiling: the provider's biggest machine.</li>
  <li>Horizontal yields per replica: 10 replicas of 1 vCPU give <strong>1,000 req/s</strong> with 10 vCPU. For that, state has to be <strong>outside the process</strong> (the session in Redis, module 5) and something has to split the requests (the load balancer, module 7). Adjusting the fleet up <strong>and down</strong>, on its own, following demand, is <strong>elasticity</strong>.</li>
  <li>The drop shows the difference that matters most. At 640 req/s, with 10 replicas each one sits at 64% (37.8 ms). <strong>1</strong> goes down: 71.1% (44.6 ms). <strong>2</strong> go down: 80% (60 ms). <strong>3</strong> go down: <strong>91.4%</strong> (126.7 ms). <strong>4</strong> go down: 6 replicas are left, capacity of 600 against 640 of demand, <strong>106.7%</strong>, and the catalog saturates.</li>
  <li>Planning calls this <strong>N+k</strong>: headroom is what is left after k replicas go down. The vertical fleet, one replica of 8 vCPU, has no headroom at all: when it goes down, <strong>100%</strong> of the capacity goes with it.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">If it goes wrong: the queue of 2,400 per minute</h3>

<p>It is 20:05, the peak is at 640 req/s and a zone failure takes down <strong>4</strong> of the 10 replicas. 6 are left, with capacity of 600 req/s, and demand is 640: <strong>40 req/s</strong> are missing. Those 40 per second become a queue, <strong>2,400 per minute</strong>, and the responses go past 2 s and get a <code>504</code>. With 7 replicas alive the catalog still held (91.4%, 126.7 ms).</p>

<p>If the store had scaled up to a single 8 vCPU replica, the outage would be worse: with no other replica, the 640 req/s meet a capacity of <strong>zero</strong>. Vertical is simpler to operate, and it concentrates the risk in a single unit.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>The two ways answer different limits.</strong> Vertical hits the machine's ceiling and the restart; horizontal asks for state outside the process and a load balancer.</li>
  <li><strong>The gain is not linear.</strong> 8 vCPU are worth 6 times (600 req/s) when 1/21 of the work is serial.</li>
  <li><strong>Headroom is the insurance.</strong> With 10 replicas, losing 3 still fits at 91.4%; losing 4 does not.</li>
  <li><strong>Elasticity cuts both ways.</strong> The fleet that goes up at the peak and down afterward is the 80 replica-hours one, not the 240 one.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the base article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Elasticity.</strong> The article says that scaling horizontally with autoscaling tools is "elasticity". Elasticity is adjusting capacity <strong>automatically</strong> to demand, <strong>in both directions</strong>, and it can be vertical or horizontal. The HPA in the next lesson is a horizontal elasticity mechanism. And "scalability", in Nygard's sense in <em>Release It!</em>, has two readings (how throughput changes with demand, and the modes of scaling); the article picks the second.</li>
    <li><strong>Optimizing the algorithm is not scaling vertically.</strong> The article puts "algorithm optimizations" inside vertical. Optimizing the code raises capacity <strong>per resource</strong> without changing the resource: it is another lever, the one in the last lesson.</li>
    <li><strong>Horizontal is not linear.</strong> The article suggests that N replicas give N times the capacity. With a shared resource, like the <code>pedidos</code> DB at 150/s or the connection pool from module 11, the gain stops (Amdahl; Gunther's Universal Scalability Law adds the coherence cost). The catalog's 10 replicas give 1,000 req/s <strong>because</strong> the catalog does not share a write DB.</li>
  </ul>

<p class="gancho">The store now knows that horizontal yields 100 req/s per replica and that headroom protects against a drop. But Black Friday does not announce the size of the peak, and turning on replicas by hand is too late. What is missing is <strong>how many</strong> replicas to turn on and <strong>when</strong>, and the formula Kubernetes uses to decide.</p>
