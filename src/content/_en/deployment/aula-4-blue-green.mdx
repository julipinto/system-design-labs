<p class="ponte">In the last lesson rolling put 2.5.0 on 100% of the traffic without anyone testing it with a customer. If a second environment exists, you can <strong>test the new version first</strong>, before it serves, and only then switch. That is <em>blue/green</em>.</p>

<OneLiner>In <strong>blue/green</strong> there are two identical environments: <strong>blue</strong> (2.4.0, serving) and <strong>green</strong> (2.5.0, no customers). You test green, and the <strong>switch</strong> is changing a pointer. Going back is switching back, as long as blue is still up.</OneLiner>

</DoIt>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>The doubled environment.</strong> <em>Green</em> has the <strong>same size</strong> as blue: 3 + 3 = 6 pods of <code>pedidos</code>. For <code>catalogo</code> on Black Friday it would be 10 + 10 = 20 pods, or <strong>10 extra replica-hours per hour</strong> of window.</li>
  <li><strong>The pointer.</strong> A Service (or a route) points to one of the two. Switching is changing the pointer, in about <strong>1 s</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>Testing without customers.</strong> Green gets smoke tests (module 24), <em>warm-up</em> and manual or automatic checks, all before the switch happens.</li>
  <li><strong>There is one database.</strong> Blue and green talk to the same <code>pg-pedidos-*</code>. The <code>frete</code> column, and both versions writing to the same table, are the subject of lesson 8: "going back to blue" does not undo what green wrote.</li>
  <li><strong>Blue stays up for a while.</strong> In Argo Rollouts <code>scaleDownDelaySeconds</code> is <strong>30 s</strong> by default (<strong>60 s</strong> in the lesson 5 lab). In that interval going back is flipping the pointer.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<p>"Instant" describes the <strong>pointer</strong>, not the flow. Connections that are already open (HTTP keep-alive, HTTP/2, long-lived gRPC, module 12) stay on the old pods until they close, and if the switch is by DNS, the TTL matters (module 1). The switch is fast; the last client leaving blue takes longer.</p>

<p>There is also the test that green allows and that decides the outcome: if the smoke test includes an order <strong>to Recife</strong>, the zero shipping shows up with 0 customers exposed. If the test does not include Recife, nobody notices, and the defect goes out with 3,600 orders in 60 minutes (R$ 54,000), as in rolling. Blue/green does not decide what you measure.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: the cold green</h3>

<p>Green is born with an <strong>empty</strong> local cache. The first request takes <strong>200 ms</strong> instead of 20 ms (example). By Little's Law (module 14), at 640 req/s: 640 × 0.2 s = <strong>128</strong> requests in flight, against 640 × 0.02 = 12.8. If each pod has <strong>10 workers</strong> (the <em>thread pool</em> from module 11), 10 pods support <strong>100</strong> in flight. Green <strong>forms a queue</strong>, with CPU at 64%, at the moment it receives 100% of the traffic.</p>

<p>The fix is <em>warm-up</em> before the switch: synthetic or mirrored traffic (lesson 9) until the cache warms up.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Testing without customers.</strong> That is the advantage of blue/green: green is validated before it receives a real order.</li>
  <li><strong>Going back in ~1 s</strong>, while blue lives. The cost is <strong>twice the pods</strong> during the window.</li>
  <li><strong>The pointer is fast; the flow is not.</strong> Open connections and the DNS TTL delay the real switch.</li>
  <li><strong>The database is shared.</strong> The environment is identical in code, not in data.</li>
</WhyItMatters>
