<p class="ponte">In the last lesson the flag drew customers by <em>bucket</em>, but a draw does not choose <strong>who</strong> should see the change first. When customers are very different (Recife is not São Paulo), the question changes: in what <strong>order</strong> does the version reach each group? The article's answer is <strong>rings</strong>, and module 16 already left the groups ready: the <strong>shards</strong>.</p>

<OneLiner>In a <strong>ring deployment</strong> the same version goes through <strong>rings</strong> of growing risk, and only moves on to the next if the previous one stays healthy for the <em>bake</em> time. The ring limits the blast radius of the <strong>change</strong>, and <strong>who exercises the code</strong> matters more than the size of the ring.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>The rings come from the shards.</strong> With <code>mkt-01</code> on <code>shard-2</code> (module 16): <strong>ring 0</strong> = <code>shard-0</code> (20% of the traffic: <code>davi-12</code>), <strong>ring 1</strong> = <code>shard-1</code> (20%: <code>lena-77</code> and <code>bia-31</code>), <strong>ring 2</strong> = <code>shard-2</code> (<strong>60%</strong>: <code>caio-58</code> and <code>mkt-01</code>).</li>
  <li><strong>The accumulated exposure grows in steps.</strong> 20% → 40% → 100%. Between one ring and the next, the <em>bake time</em>: the time to wait for whatever the defect would take to show up (AWS Builders' Library).</li>
  <li><strong>Ring 0 is the <em>one-box</em>.</strong> AWS starts with a single box, to catch the defect with the smallest possible radius. But the shipping defect belongs to <strong>Recife</strong>, and Lena is on <code>shard-1</code>: ring 0 (<code>davi-12</code>, SP) <strong>sees nothing</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>The defect shows up in ring 1,</strong> with <strong>40%</strong> of the traffic already on 2.5.0. Ring 0 passed clean and gave a false sense of safety.</li>
  <li><strong>The fix is to put synthetic customers</strong> with orders in <strong>every</strong> city into ring 0 (the <em>one-box</em> idea): Recife's zero shipping shows up already in ring 0, with no real customer exposed.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">The cost, and the choice of the first ring</h3>

<p>Rings require <strong>pods per ring</strong>: the 3 <code>pedidos</code> pods become 1 per shard, of 30 orders/s each. <code>shard-2</code>, with 60% of 30 orders/s, sees 18/s and fits. With Black Friday doubled, 60 × 60% = <strong>36/s</strong> goes over 30: the hot ring needs more pods. It also requires <strong>one version per ring</strong> to operate and monitor. The article warns about this cost, and it connects with the <strong>bulkhead</strong> (module 25: the ring is the blast radius of the <em>change</em>) and with <strong>cells</strong> (module 26).</p>

<p>The article proposes choosing the first ring by customer <strong>cluster</strong>, with <em>k-means</em> over segment, volume and region. <em>k-means</em> only <strong>discovers</strong> similar groups from variables; it does <strong>not say</strong> which ones are low risk. In practice, the first ring comes from a <strong>risk and business criterion</strong> (employees, synthetic customers, small regions), and clustering helps in the second step, it is not a prerequisite.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: the last ring has 60%</h3>

<p>The step from <strong>40% to 100%</strong> is the biggest and the riskiest. If the defect only shows up under <code>mkt-01</code>'s load, it only blows up in ring 2, when 60% of the traffic changes at once. The fix is to split ring 2 (the dedicated <code>shard-2</code> from module 16) or to add an intermediate ring, so that no step goes beyond a fraction the store can afford to lose.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>The ring is a chosen blast radius.</strong> The change only reaches the next group after the previous one stays healthy.</li>
  <li><strong>Who exercises the code matters more than the size.</strong> Davi's ring 0 does not see Recife; synthetic customers in every city do.</li>
  <li><strong>The big step is the weak point.</strong> 40% → 100% concentrates the risk in the last ring.</li>
  <li><strong>It costs pods and operations.</strong> One ring per shard means more idle capacity and more versions to monitor.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the base article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>"Sharding Deployment."</strong> The most common name is <strong>ring deployment</strong> (or <em>waves</em>); AWS and Microsoft describe rings and waves with <em>bake time</em>. The article is right about the cost; it should add that <strong>global</strong> dependencies (configuration, authentication, catalog) bring back the global blast radius (module 25).</li>
    <li><strong>Clustering with k-means.</strong> It discovers groups, not risk; synthetic customers in every city are worth more than a cluster.</li>
    <li><strong>What the article is missing.</strong> N/N−1 compatibility in <strong>events</strong> and APIs (tolerant reader) and the change freeze before events like Black Friday.</li>
    <li><strong>References.</strong> The Medium text on k-means was not checked and stays out; those from Split.io, Red Hat, Codefresh and Spacelift are vendor blogs and enter only as "go deeper".</li>
  </ul>
