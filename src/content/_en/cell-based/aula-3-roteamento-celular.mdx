<p class="ponte">In the last lesson Lena entered <code>celula-recife</code> and took the last two cats there. Someone had to know, <strong>before</strong> the door, that <code>lena-77</code> lives in Recife. That someone is the <strong>router</strong>, the only part of the store that all the cells share.</p>

<OneLiner>The router has to be <strong>thin</strong> (only route), <strong>deterministic</strong> (the same key, the same cell) and <strong>more available than the cells</strong>. If it is not, it becomes the new single point of failure.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>The arrival.</strong> DNS takes <code>api.loja.com.br</code> to the nearest <em>edge cell</em> (<code>edge-loja</code>, 3 regional instances: SP, Rio and Recife).</li>
  <li><strong>The key.</strong> The edge reads the <code>cliente_id</code> in the JWT (module 8): <code>lena-77</code>. It does not open the order body or know what a cat is.</li>
  <li><strong>The map.</strong> A lookup in <code>mapa-v42</code>, which lives <strong>in memory</strong> on the edge itself (1.5 million customers × ~100 B = <strong>150 MB</strong>), returns <code>celula-recife</code>.</li>
  <li><strong>The handoff.</strong> The request goes on to the cell's <code>gw</code> (lesson 2). From there the edge is out of the way.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<p>The map does not appear on its own. Another part, the <strong>control plane</strong>, decides the <strong>placement</strong> (which cell a new customer goes into), the <strong>migration</strong> (moving a customer between cells) and <strong>publishes</strong> the map. It stays <strong>off the order path</strong>: if it goes down, the edge keeps routing with the last map.</p>

<h3 class="sec">The key and the map</h3>

<p>The key has to be <strong>stable</strong> and tied to the business: <code>cliente_id</code>, <code>conta_id</code>, <code>tenant_id</code>. To tie it to the cell there are <strong>three ways</strong>, which module 16 already showed for the shard:</p>

<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Registry table.</strong> One row <code>cliente_id → cell</code>. The 150 MB map. Moving a customer is editing a row.</li>
  <li><strong><code>hash mod N</code>.</strong> No table, but every change in N <strong>reshuffles</strong> almost everything.</li>
  <li><strong>Ring (consistent hash).</strong> Each cell takes points on the ring and a new member only takes a slice.</li>
</ol>

<p>The difference shows up when the store goes from 4 to <strong>5 cells</strong>. In a simulation with 100,000 keys, <code>hash mod N</code> moves <strong>80.1%</strong> of them (3 → 4 moves 75.0%; with <code>murmur3</code> module 16 measured 75.2%). The ring moves about <strong>20%</strong> (theory: 1 ÷ (N + 1); module 16 measured 25.7% for 3 → 4). The table moves <strong>only the ones you move</strong>. This is the article's <em>rehash storm</em>: <code>mod N</code> would make 80% of the customers change cell, and the data migrates with them.</p>

<p>Moving a customer with the table has a data cost. <code>mkt-01</code> has 20 million rows of 2 KB: <strong>40 GB</strong>. At 50 MB/s that is <strong>800 s = 13 min 20 s</strong> (the module 16 math): copy, apply what arrived during the copy and <strong>flip the map</strong>. Cheaper than 80% of the store, but not free.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Edge cells: thin, not "smart"</h3>

<p>The router runs where the request passes: DNS, gateway, edge <em>proxy</em> or mesh (module 10). The article asks for a "smart and agnostic" layer that goes "beyond a proxy". AWS asks for the <strong>opposite</strong>: the <strong>thinnest possible</strong> layer, with no business logic, simple enough to be <strong>tested in full</strong>. The text follows AWS: any logic at the edge is logic that goes down with all four cells.</p>

<p>The article also says the edge should be "as <em>stateless</em> as possible, but it can have registry state". The state is the <strong>map</strong>: small, in memory and with <strong>static stability</strong>. If the control plane dies, the edges keep going with the last map they received, without asking anyone for anything to continue.</p>

<p>And the single-point math shows up. In <strong>series</strong>, a router at 99.99% and a cell at 99.9% give 0.9999 × 0.999 = <strong>99.89%</strong>. The router is the <strong>ceiling</strong> for all the cells, so it has to be more available than they are and have several instances.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Synchronous cells</h3>

<p>On the synchronous path the routing is on the <strong>critical path</strong>, and the cell's latency is what the customer <strong>feels</strong>. That is why each cell is its <strong>own</strong> unit of capacity: CPU, memory, database connection pool, <em>thread pools</em> and <em>rate limit</em> are configured <strong>per cell</strong>, not globally. The <code>mkt-01</code> cap of 100 req/s (module 25) is, in practice, the size of <code>celula-mkt</code>.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Asynchronous cells</h3>

<p>In the async case the routing happens before the topic. An <strong>edge consumer</strong> reads a domain topic and <strong>republishes</strong> each message on the cell's topic, which only knows its own messaging. That is the path of the PSP webhook: it arrives at <strong>a single address</strong>, and the edge reads the cell from the <strong>charge reference</strong>. The SP Pix charge, <code>c-7733</code>, carries <code>celula=sp</code> and goes to the <code>celula-sp</code> topic.</p>

<p>The Kafka poison shows the gain. On a <strong>shared</strong> <code>pedidos</code> topic (3 partitions, 4 events per order: 30 orders/s = 120 events/s, 40/s per partition, module 13), the consumer <strong>crashes on deserialization</strong>, before reading the DLQ policy. The poison's partition <strong>gets stuck</strong>. In <strong>10 min (600 s)</strong> there are <strong>24,000</strong> events stuck (40 × 600), and <strong>60% (14,400)</strong> are from individual customers: Lena's <code>PedidoPago</code>, delayed.</p>

<p>With <strong>per-cell</strong> topics, <code>pedidos.mkt</code> receives 12 × 4 ÷ 3 = <strong>16 events/s per partition</strong>: <strong>9,600</strong> stuck, <strong>all from the partner</strong>. The other three cells keep going with a lag of <strong>0</strong>, and the <em>backlog</em> stays in the cell that produced it.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: the bad map</h3>

<p><code>mapa-v43</code> ships with a typo: <code>bia-31 → celula-xx</code>, a cell that does not exist. The control plane publishes the file to <strong>all the edges at once</strong>, and whoever looks up the map gets a <code>503</code>. The config is <strong>shared</strong> and the radius is back to <strong>100%</strong>, the same problem as in lesson 1 by another route.</p>

<p>The way out is the deploy one: <strong>validate</strong> the map first (every key points to a live cell), publish <strong>per edge</strong>, in rings, and keep the <strong>last good map</strong> to roll back to. Static stability protects against the control plane going down, but not against a bad map it published itself.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>The router is the shared part that is left.</strong> Thin, deterministic and more available than the cells, or it becomes the new single point of failure.</li>
  <li><strong>The registry table does not cause a <em>rehash storm</em>.</strong> From 4 to 5 cells: 80.1% (<code>mod N</code>), ~20% (ring) and 0 (table).</li>
  <li><strong>The per-cell topic keeps the backlog.</strong> 24,000 stuck events become 9,600, and the 14,400 from individual customers become 0.</li>
  <li><strong>The map is shared config.</strong> Publishing to all the edges at once brings back the 100% radius.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <ul>
    <li><strong>"Smart edge cells".</strong> The article asks for a "smart and agnostic" router that goes beyond a basic <em>proxy</em>. AWS asks for the thinnest possible layer, with no business logic, testable in full. The text follows AWS.</li>
