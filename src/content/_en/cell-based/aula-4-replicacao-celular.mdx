<p class="ponte">In the last lesson the <code>mkt-01</code> poison took down <code>celula-mkt</code> and the router kept going with the other three. But what if the cell goes down <strong>for real</strong>, from a server, a bad deploy or a disk? Its customers need somewhere to go, and the data has to be there already.</p>

<OneLiner>Each cell's data has <strong>a copy in a passive cell</strong>, <strong>asynchronous</strong> by default (the loss is <strong>rate × lag</strong>). The <strong>consistent</strong> form (2PC) adds up the latency and the availability of the cells, and <em>shuffle sharding</em> gives each customer <strong>k cells</strong> instead of one.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>The passive.</strong> Each active cell has a <strong>passive cell</strong> in another region: SP → Rio (300 ms lag), Rio → SP (300 ms), Recife → SP (800 ms) and <code>mkt</code> → SP (800 ms).</li>
  <li><strong>The <em>stream</em>.</strong> The active's database sends its log to the passive, off the order path. The customer's commit stays <strong>local, in 2 ms</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>The outage.</strong> The active cell goes down. The passive promotes its database and the router swaps the map row.</li>
  <li><strong>The loss.</strong> What was in flight within the lag window, <strong>rate × lag</strong>, did not arrive.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">Asynchronous replication: what is lost</h3>

<p>Since the <em>stream</em> is asynchronous, the orders from the last 300 ms (or 800 ms) may not have arrived when the active goes down. The expected loss is <strong>rate × lag</strong> (module 17):</p>

<div class="sr-tablewrap">
<table class="sr-table">
  <thead><tr><th>Cell</th><th>Orders/s</th><th>Passive in</th><th>Lag</th><th>Expected loss</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><th><code>celula-sp</code></th><td>9.9</td><td>Rio</td><td>300 ms</td><td><strong>2.97</strong> orders</td></tr>
    <tr><th><code>celula-rio</code></th><td>4.5</td><td>SP</td><td>300 ms</td><td><strong>1.35</strong></td></tr>
    <tr><th><code>celula-recife</code></th><td>3.6</td><td>SP</td><td>800 ms</td><td><strong>2.88</strong></td></tr>
    <tr><th><code>celula-mkt</code></th><td>12.0</td><td>SP</td><td>800 ms</td><td><strong>9.6</strong></td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
</div>

<p>Before the cells, with a single primary at 30 orders/s, the Rio replica lost <strong>9</strong> (30 × 0.3) and the Recife one <strong>24</strong> (30 × 0.8). The cell reduces the loss in orders because it reduces the <strong>rate per primary</strong>, not because the lag got better.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Consistent replication: 2PC</h3>

<p>Where losing an order hurts, the article proposes <em>Two-Phase Commit</em>: the transaction only counts when <strong>all</strong> the cells confirm. There are two rounds (<em>prepare</em> and <em>commit</em>), and each waits for the slowest participant. The times below are a <strong>model</strong> (network rounds), not a measurement:</p>

<div class="sr-tablewrap">
<table class="sr-table">
  <thead><tr><th>Mode</th><th>Commit latency</th><th>Hot row (1 ÷ latency)</th><th>Availability (0.999<sup>k</sup>)</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><th>Asynchronous (active only)</th><td><strong>2 ms</strong></td><td>500 reservations/s</td><td>99.9%</td></tr>
    <tr><th>2PC SP + Rio</th><td>2 × 10 + 2 × 2 = <strong>24 ms</strong></td><td>41.7/s</td><td>99.8%</td></tr>
    <tr><th>2PC SP + Rio + Recife</th><td>2 × 80 + 2 × 2 = <strong>164 ms</strong></td><td>6.1/s</td><td>99.7%</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
</div>

<p>The <strong>hot row</strong> is the cat's stock (module 11): the <em>lock</em> is held during the rounds, so only one reservation gets in every 24 ms (or 164 ms). And any participant being down <strong>aborts</strong> the transaction, which is why availability becomes 0.999<sup>k</sup>.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Headroom for the <em>failover</em></h3>

<p>When a cell goes down, the others <strong>take over its load</strong>. With N <strong>equal</strong> cells, each survivor gets 1 ÷ (N − 1) more. To stay at ρ ≤ 70% (module 23) after <strong>one</strong> outage, the <strong>normal</strong> occupancy has a ceiling: 70% × (N − 1) ÷ N.</p>

<div class="sr-tablewrap">
<table class="sr-table">
  <thead><tr><th>N cells</th><th>Increase per survivor</th><th>Maximum normal occupancy</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><th>2</th><td>+100%</td><td><strong>35%</strong></td></tr>
    <tr><th>3</th><td>+50%</td><td><strong>46.7%</strong></td></tr>
    <tr><th>4</th><td>+33.3%</td><td><strong>52.5%</strong></td></tr>
    <tr><th>10</th><td>+11.1%</td><td><strong>63.0%</strong></td></tr>
    <tr><th>20</th><td>+5.3%</td><td><strong>66.5%</strong></td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
</div>

<p>Active-passive pairs (N = 2) force you to run at <strong>35%</strong> of capacity. Many small cells run close to the target, which is why the floor from lesson 2 and the number of cells pull in opposite directions.</p>

<h3 class="sec"><em>Shuffle sharding</em>: the radius as a probability</h3>

<p>Instead of one fixed cell, each customer sits in <strong>k cells</strong> drawn from N, stable and with the data replicated in them. A cell that goes down only affects those who have it in their set, and those who have it <strong>still have the other</strong>. The question stops being "what is the impact of a <em>shard</em> failing" and becomes "what is the chance of a customer being in <strong>exactly</strong> the cells that went down".</p>

<p>The article gives the formula <code>(f/N)^k</code>, with f cells down. It is an <strong>approximation</strong>, because it draws <strong>with replacement</strong>. The exact math for "the customer has all its k cells among the f that went down" is <strong>C(f,k) ÷ C(N,k)</strong>, which for f = k is 1 ÷ C(N,k):</p>

<div class="sr-tablewrap">
<table class="sr-table">
  <thead><tr><th>N</th><th>f = k</th><th>Article (f/N)<sup>k</sup></th><th>Exact 1 ÷ C(N,k)</th><th>Pairs/sets</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><th>20</th><td>2</td><td>1%</td><td><strong>0.526%</strong></td><td>190</td></tr>
    <tr><th>20</th><td>3</td><td>0.3375% (the article prints 0.33%)</td><td><strong>0.0877%</strong></td><td>1,140</td></tr>
    <tr><th>20</th><td>5</td><td><strong>0.0977%</strong> (the article prints 0.009%)</td><td><strong>0.00645%</strong></td><td>15,504</td></tr>
    <tr><th>10</th><td>2</td><td>4%</td><td><strong>2.22%</strong> (the 1 ÷ 45 from module 16)</td><td>45</td></tr>
    <tr><th>4</th><td>2</td><td>25%</td><td><strong>16.7%</strong></td><td>6</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
</div>

<p>The article's 3rd row has an error: (5 ÷ 20)<sup>5</sup> is <strong>0.0977%</strong>, not 0.009% (10× smaller). With <strong>4 cells <em>shuffle</em> barely helps</strong> (6 pairs); with 10 it becomes the 2.2% from module 16; with 20, 0.53%.</p>

<p>With 10 cells (c1…c10, k = 2, <em>rendezvous</em> draw with <code>murmur3</code>) the five customers end up like this: <code>mkt-01</code> <strong>{'{8, 9}'}</strong>, <code>lena-77</code> <strong>{'{6, 8}'}</strong>, <code>bia-31</code> <strong>{'{4, 6}'}</strong>, <code>caio-58</code> <strong>{'{1, 4}'}</strong>, <code>davi-12</code> <strong>{'{1, 9}'}</strong>. The <code>mkt-01</code> poison takes down cells 8 and 9. Bia and Caio <strong>do not even notice</strong>. Lena and Davi lose <strong>one</strong> and keep the other. Only whoever has <strong>exactly</strong> {'{8, 9}'} (1 in 45, 2.2%) is left without service, and that is <code>mkt-01</code> itself. The 8 survivors take on +25% (2 ÷ 8) each.</p>

<Widget name="shuffle" title="Exact × approximate shuffle" hint="N (4 to 50), k (1 to 5) and f (1 to k + 3). Shows (f/N)^k (the article), C(f,k) ÷ C(N,k) (exact), the number of sets and the five drawn customers (N = 10); flags the error in the article's 3rd row (0.009% × 0.0977%)." />

<p>The article compares: "for 1% you would need 100 <em>bulkheads</em>". But it mixes <strong>one</strong> failure (100 fixed compartments) with <strong>two</strong> (20 cells, k = 2). In the same failure, <strong>one</strong> cell down leaves <strong>0%</strong> of customers fully out (all have another copy), and <strong>two</strong> leave 0.526%, which equals <strong>190</strong> fixed compartments, not 100.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: the passive cannot cope</h3>

<p><code>celula-sp</code> has 33% of the traffic and, if it goes down, <strong>only</strong> the passive in Rio takes it over. Rio goes from <strong>15% to 48%</strong> of the traffic: from 4.5 to <strong>14.4 orders/s</strong>, or <strong>3.2×</strong>, not the +33% from the equal-cells table. The ways out cost different things:</p>

<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Run at 35%</strong> in active-passive pairs: you pay in idle capacity.</li>
  <li><strong>Many small cells</strong>, which dilute the increase (+11.1% with 10): you pay in floor per cell.</li>
  <li><strong>Degrade by priority</strong> (the 12 · 6 · 2 lanes from module 25): checkout stays and reprocessing waits.</li>
</ol>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>The loss is rate × lag.</strong> 2.97 · 1.35 · 2.88 · 9.6 orders: the cell reduces the rate per primary, not the lag.</li>
  <li><strong>2PC adds up.</strong> 24 ms and 164 ms of commit, 41.7 and 6.1 reservations/s on a hot row, 99.8% and 99.7% availability.</li>
  <li><strong>Headroom is a function of N.</strong> 35% with 2 cells, 66.5% with 20; unequal cells make it worse (Rio 15% → 48%).</li>
  <li><strong><em>Shuffle</em> turns the radius into a probability.</strong> 0.526% with 20, 2.2% with 10, 16.7% with 4. The article's math is approximate.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={5}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">The data poison is replicated along with it</h4>
  <p>The <strong>request</strong> poison (the <code>quantidade: 2147483648</code>) stays in the cell: it takes down the process and the queue that received it. The <strong>data</strong> poison is different. A <strong>corrupted row</strong> in the active's database is replicated to the passive, and when the active goes down because of it, the passive reads the same row and goes down too. Passive replication protects against infrastructure failure, not against bad data.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the base article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>(f/N)<sup>k</sup> is an approximation.</strong> The exact one is C(f,k) ÷ C(N,k): 0.526% (not 1%) for N = 20 and f = k = 2. The 3rd row of the article's table is wrong (0.0977%, not 0.009%); the 2nd row (0.33%) is the formula, and the exact value is 0.0877%.</li>
    <li><strong>"1% with 20 cells = 100 <em>bulkheads</em>".</strong> It compares one failure with two. In the same failure, one cell down leaves 0% of customers out, and two leave 0.526%, the equivalent of 190 compartments.</li>
    <li><strong>2PC across stacks.</strong> The article acknowledges the coupling, but 2PC <strong>blocks</strong> (if the coordinator disappears after <em>prepare</em>, the participants are left with locked rows, as Fowler says) and <strong>adds up</strong> the latency and the availability of the cells. AWS says the cell shares no state. What is actually used is async; for "zero RPO" where it hurts, use synchronous replication of <strong>a single database</strong> (module 17: 12 ms with Rio, 82 ms with Recife) or consensus (Raft or Paxos), not 2PC across stacks.</li>
    <li><strong>What was not run.</strong> No 2PC in PostgreSQL: the 24 and 164 ms times are a <strong>model</strong> of network rounds. The <em>shuffle sharding</em> article cited comes through a translator (<code>translate.goog</code>); the AWS original goes in instead.</li>
  </ul>
