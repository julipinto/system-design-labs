<p class="ponte">In the last lesson <code>estoque-1</code> reached 500 orders per second because 90% of each order was waiting. <code>relatorios</code> is the opposite. Every day at 23:00 it closes the day by reading 400 thousand rows of <code>itens_pedido</code> and spends the whole time calculating, without waiting for anyone. For that work, interleaving orders does not help; what's missing is another core. That is parallelism.</p>

<OneLiner>Parallelism is running parts of the work <strong>at the same time</strong>, each on its own core; it pays off for work that uses CPU, but the part that cannot be split limits the gain.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><code>relatorios</code> runs on a <strong>4 vCPU</strong> pod. At 23:00 it reads the day's <strong>400 thousand rows</strong> and sums values per SKU. Each row spends CPU, and together they spend <strong>40 s</strong> on a single core. It waits for no one, so the concurrency from lesson 2 does not help: one core, 40 s.</li>
  <li>The work splits well, because each row is independent. The program cuts the 400 thousand into <strong>4 slices of 100 thousand</strong> and hands one to each core. The 4 run at the same time, and each takes 40 s ÷ 4 = <strong>10 s</strong>.</li>
  <li>After the slices comes what does not split: adding up the 4 partial results and saving the day's summary, <strong>2 s</strong> on a single core. The total is 10 s + 2 s = <strong>12 s</strong>, against 40 s + 2 s = <strong>42 s</strong> sequentially.</li>
  <li>The speedup is 42 ÷ 12 = <strong>3.5×</strong>, not 4×. Amdahl's law explains it: the serial part is 2 ÷ 42 = <strong>4.8%</strong>, and it does not shrink with more cores. The formula is 1 ÷ (0.048 + 0.952 ÷ N). With 4 cores it gives 3.5; with 8 it gives <strong>6.0</strong>, not 8; with infinite cores the ceiling is 21×.</li>
  <li>The repository's lab uses the same idea with a barbecue: 16 tasks sliced among 8 "friends" (<code>numCPU = 8</code>, <code>sliceSize = 2</code>). Friend 1 takes picanha + ribs and needs <strong>12 s</strong>; friend 3 takes chilling the beer + organizing the fridge and needs <strong>2 s</strong>. The sum of the 8 is 12+5+2+5+7+4+6+8 = <strong>49 s</strong>. The total time is the slowest one's, 12 s, and the speedup is 49 ÷ 12 = <strong>4.1×</strong> on 8 "CPUs", an efficiency of 51%.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">An honest warning: the lab does not prove CPU parallelism</h3>

<p>Both of the lab's tasks use <code>time.Sleep</code>. Sleeping does not spend CPU, so the same program would take the <strong>same 12 s on a single core</strong>. What the lab shows is overlapping waits, the concurrency from lesson 2, just written with 8 goroutines. Real parallelism shows up in <code>relatorios</code>, where each slice calculates: without 4 cores, the 12 s become 42.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: slicing by count and not by time</h3>

<p>The lab slices by <strong>number of tasks</strong>: 2 for each friend. But the tasks do not cost the same. Friend 1 gets the two heaviest (12 s) and friend 3 the two lightest (2 s). Six friends finish and wait for friend 1. The ideal would be to divide by <strong>time</strong>: 49 s ÷ 8 ≈ <strong>6.1 s</strong> per friend, twice the speed. In <code>relatorios</code> dividing by count works, because every row costs the same, 0.1 ms. When the cost varies, slice by cost, or let the cores pick up work from a queue.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Parallelism is for CPU.</strong> If the work only waits, concurrency already solves it; if it calculates, it needs cores.</li>
  <li><strong>The gain is never N×.</strong> The serial part (the 2 s of summing and saving) is a ceiling: with 4.8% of it, 4 cores give 3.5× and 8 give 6.0×.</li>
  <li><strong>An uneven slice wastes a core.</strong> The total time is the slowest slice's, not the average.</li>
  <li><strong>Test with work that uses CPU.</strong> A lab with <code>Sleep</code> looks parallel and is not.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the source article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong><code>time.Sleep</code> hides what the example claims to show.</strong> The lab demonstrates overlapping waits; CPU parallelism needs work that calculates, like <code>relatorios</code>.</li>
    <li><strong><code>runtime.NumCPU()</code> inside a pod.</strong> It reads the CPUs of the <em>machine</em>, not the container's limit. On a 1 vCPU pod on a 16-core node, it returns 16, and the program creates too many slices. The default <code>GOMAXPROCS</code> started respecting the cgroup CPU limit from Go 1.25 on, but <code>NumCPU()</code> still returns the machine's CPUs. In a pod, read <code>runtime.GOMAXPROCS(0)</code> (or use the pod's limit) to decide the number of slices.</li>
  </ul>

<p class="gancho"><code>relatorios</code> parallelized inside one pod, with 4 threads sharing the same memory. But there is another way to run things at the same time: start more pods, each with its own memory, like <code>estoque-1</code> and <code>estoque-2</code>. What's missing is where each kind of parallelism lives, and what breaks when the work goes from one process to several.</p>
