<p class="ponte">In the last lesson the <code>sync.Mutex</code> held Caio back for 2 ms and the cat went to Bia. But that was with one replica. Black Friday brings up <code>estoque-2</code>, the load balancer sends Bia to <code>estoque-1</code> and Caio to <code>estoque-2</code>, and each replica has its own mutex, in its own memory. Both read 1, both decrement, and the stock goes to −1 again. The local mutex only protects inside the process, and the lock has to start holding across processes.</p>

<OneLiner>A distributed lock is a lock kept in a place that all replicas can see (Redis, ZooKeeper); since it can expire on its own, whoever holds it can lose it without knowing, and that is why, for the last cat, the final check belongs to the database.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>The lock leaves memory and goes to Redis, with a single command: <code>SET lock:estoque:GATO-042 &lt;token&gt; NX PX 5000</code>. The <code>NX</code> creates the key <strong>only if it does not exist</strong>, and the <code>PX 5000</code> already creates it with a <strong>5 s TTL</strong>. Since it is an atomic command, two requests at the same time do not get through together.</li>
  <li>Bia runs the <code>SET</code> and gets <code>OK</code> at t = 1 ms. Caio runs his and gets <code>nil</code> at t = 3 ms: the key exists, the lock is Bia's. He tries again <strong>every 50 ms</strong>.</li>
  <li>Bia releases the lock at t = 6 ms. Caio's attempt at t = 53 ms works and he gets in at t = 54 ms, reads <strong>0</strong> and gets <code>409</code> at t ≈ 56 ms. He releases the lock at t = 57 ms.</li>
  <li>Whoever releases checks the owner before deleting, with the Lua script from Redis's own docs: <code>if redis.call("get",KEYS[1]) == ARGV[1] then return redis.call("del",KEYS[1]) else return 0 end</code>. The token is different on each replica, and only the owner deletes.</li>
  <li>The cost changed. The lock cycle is now 6 ms: 1 ms for the <code>SET</code>, 4 ms of work and 1 ms for the <code>DEL</code>. The ceiling drops to 1 s ÷ 6 ms = <strong>166 reservations per second</strong> of the same SKU, against the 250 of the local mutex.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">The lab's other example: ZooKeeper</h3>

<p>ZooKeeper keeps the lock as an <em>ephemeral znode</em>: it disappears when the session of whoever created it ends. An example: <code>/locks/estoque-GATO-042/lock-0000000007</code>. The official recipe uses <strong>ephemeral and sequential</strong> nodes: each replica creates its own, the one with the lowest number holds the lock, and the others watch only the <strong>previous</strong> node. That gives a fair queue and avoids the "herd" of everyone waking up when the lock is released. If <code>estoque-1</code> dies, the session expires (for example, in 10 s) and the lock goes away on its own.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: the lock expires and the owner does not know</h3>

<p>This is the center of the lesson. <code>estoque-1</code> takes the lock (token <code>a1</code>, TTL of 5 s) and <strong>stops for 6 s</strong> before the <code>UPDATE</code>: a long garbage-collection pause, a bad network, anything. At t = 5.001 s the TTL expires and the key goes away. Caio tries at t = 3, 53, 103 ms, once every 50 ms, and only gives up at 10 s. He takes the lock on the first attempt after the expiry, at t ≈ <strong>5.003 s</strong>, with token <code>b2</code>, reads 1, decrements to 0 and buys.</p>

<p>Bia wakes up at t = 6 s and keeps thinking the lock is hers. Her <code>UPDATE</code> takes the stock to −1. Worse: the lab's plain <code>DEL</code> deletes the key of whoever holds it at that moment, without checking whose it is. The lab already shows the risk: <code>processaMensagem</code> sleeps <strong>10 s</strong> and the TTL is <strong>10 s</strong>, so the lock expires at the very moment the work ends.</p>

<p>There are three remedies, and they add up:</p>

<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>A token per owner, with compare-and-delete.</strong> Each replica writes a value that is only its own and only deletes the key if the value is still its own. It avoids deleting someone else's lock, but does not stop the late <code>UPDATE</code>.</li>
  <li><strong>Fencing token</strong> (Kleppmann). The lock service returns a number that only grows: 33 for Bia, 34 for Caio. The database refuses a write with a number lower than the highest already seen, and Bia's <code>UPDATE</code> (33) is dropped because 34 already went through. Redis's <code>SET NX</code> does not give this number; ZooKeeper does (the node's sequence number).</li>
  <li><strong>The <code>UPDATE ... AND quantidade &gt; 0</code> from lesson 7.</strong> The database decides, <strong>without depending on a clock</strong>. Bia's <code>UPDATE</code>, on waking up, finds the quantity at 0 and affects 0 rows.</li>
</ol>

<p>Kleppmann separates two uses of the lock. The <strong>efficiency</strong> lock avoids repeated work: if it fails, it costs a little, and a simple Redis will do. The <strong>correctness</strong> lock protects the data: if it fails, the cat is sold twice, and it needs fencing or a check in the database. For the last cat, the database solves it. The distributed lock is for what <strong>does not fit in a transaction</strong>: the 23:00 close running only once (the failure from lesson 4) and a message being consumed only once (module 13).</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>A local mutex does not cross processes.</strong> Two replicas, two mutexes, the double sale comes back.</li>
  <li><strong>The TTL is a bet.</strong> It solves the owner who dies and creates the owner who still thinks it has the lock.</li>
  <li><strong>A correctness lock needs more than the lock.</strong> A fencing token, or a check in the database that does not depend on a clock.</li>
  <li><strong>Use the right tool.</strong> Last cat: <code>UPDATE ... AND quantidade &gt; 0</code>. 23:00 close: distributed lock.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <h4 class="deep-h">Redlock: the two positions</h4>
  <p>Redlock tries to give more of a guarantee by using 5 independent Redis instances: the lock only counts if the majority agree. Kleppmann (2016) argues that it depends on timing assumptions that do not hold in practice (clocks and pauses) and that it does not generate a fencing token. Salvatore Sanfilippo, the author of Redis, answered in "Is Redlock safe?" that the assumptions are reasonable. Both readings are published, and it is worth reading both.</p>

  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the source article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Redis.</strong> A <code>GET</code> followed by a <code>SET</code> is not atomic: another process gets in between the two. The lab fixes the second step with <code>SetNX</code>, but <strong>keeps the <code>GET</code> before it</strong>. The value is also the string <code>"locked"</code>, the same for everyone, so without a token per owner the <code>DEL</code> deletes someone else's lock. A 10 s TTL for 10 s of work lets the lock expire in the middle, and there is no fencing token. The right command is a single one: <code>SET key token NX PX ttl</code>. Plain <code>SETNX</code> has been deprecated since Redis 2.6.12.</li>
    <li><strong>ZooKeeper.</strong> The <code>Exists</code> before the <code>Create</code> is not atomic either: the <code>Create</code> already fails with <code>ErrNodeExists</code>, and it alone is enough. The article shows a 10 s session and the log shows <code>timeout=40000</code>, because the server negotiates the value; the lab uses <code>1000*time.Second</code>. The official recipe uses <strong>sequential</strong> nodes, and the lab uses a fixed node, with no queue and with the "herd".</li>
    <li><strong>"A distributed mutex is, in some ways, easier than in shared memory."</strong> It is harder. There is no common clock, the owner can get slow without knowing (garbage-collection pauses, network) and the lock can expire while it still thinks it has it.</li>
  </ul>
