<p class="ponte">In the last lesson <code>relatorios</code> gained speed by splitting 400 thousand rows into 4 slices, one per core of the same pod. But the store already does another kind of thing at the same time: <code>estoque-1</code> and <code>estoque-2</code> serve orders together, each in its own pod. Both are called parallelism, and they behave very differently when they need to agree on something.</p>

<OneLiner><strong>Internal</strong> parallelism happens inside a process, with threads that share memory; <strong>external</strong> parallelism happens between processes, pods or machines, each with its own memory.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Internal.</strong> The parallelism is inside one process, and it is the <strong>code</strong> that creates it: <code>relatorios</code> cuts the rows into 4 slices and hands them to 4 threads. The 4 share the memory of the same pod and go up together with it. The limit is the number of cores the pod has.</li>
  <li><strong>External.</strong> The parallelism is between processes, and it is the <strong>infrastructure</strong> that creates it: <code>estoque-1</code> and <code>estoque-2</code> are 2 processes, in 2 pods, with 2 memories. The load balancer from module 7 splits the orders between them. The limit is the number of pods the cluster sustains (and what exists behind them, like the database).</li>
  <li>The same design shows up in other places. The consumers of a message queue (module 13) are external parallelism: each one is a process that takes messages from the same place. The article also mentions Hadoop and Spark, which split a big calculation across many machines.</li>
  <li><strong>Coordination</strong> is where the two part ways. In internal, whoever needs to agree on something uses the shared memory: a lock (<code>mutex</code>, lesson 7). In external, the memory is different, and the agreement has to go through something both can see: the database, Redis, ZooKeeper (lesson 8).</li>
  <li>External also has another gain: <strong>failure isolation</strong>. If a <code>panic</code> takes down <code>estoque-1</code> (lesson 1), <code>estoque-2</code> stays up. Inside a single process, a goroutine that crashes takes all of them along.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: the close runs twice</h3>

<p><code>relatorios</code> gets a second replica to handle Black Friday. The scheduler that triggers the close at 23:00 lives <em>inside</em> the program, and now there are two programs. At 23:00 both replicas wake up, each one reads the 400 thousand rows, each one calculates and each one saves the day's summary. The result: the summary is saved <strong>2 times</strong>, and the day's revenue shows up doubled.</p>

<p>Nobody made a mistake in one replica's code: each one does exactly what it should. What was missing was coordination <strong>between</strong> processes, and an in-memory <code>mutex</code> does not help, because each replica has its own. The solution is a lock that both can see, the subject of lesson 8.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Two places, two tools.</strong> An in-memory lock solves it inside the process; between processes you need something from outside.</li>
  <li><strong>Adding replicas changes the program.</strong> What worked with 1 pod (the scheduler, the <code>mutex</code>) can break with 2.</li>
  <li><strong>Scaling sideways isolates failures.</strong> A <code>panic</code> in one replica does not take down the others.</li>
  <li><strong>The limit changes.</strong> In internal, it is the pod's cores; in external, it is what exists behind the pods, like the database.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={4}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Taking the scheduler out of the program</h4>
  <p>One way out of the duplicated close is not to keep the scheduler inside the replicas. A Kubernetes <code>CronJob</code> triggers a single Job at 23:00, and the <code>concurrencyPolicy: Forbid</code> policy prevents a second run while the first has not finished. The documentation itself warns that, in rare cases, a CronJob can create two Jobs or none, so the close still needs to be safe if it runs twice (for example, saving the day's summary with a unique key).</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Four cores in one pod against four pods with one core</h4>
  <p>It gives the same number of cores, but not the same behavior. With 4 pods, memory is not shared (an in-memory cache is copied 4 times), coordination goes through the network, and an outage takes only a quarter of the capacity. With 1 pod of 4 cores, exchanging data is cheap and an outage takes everything.</p>
