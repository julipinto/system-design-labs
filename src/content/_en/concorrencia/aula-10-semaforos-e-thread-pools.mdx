<p class="ponte">In the last lesson the spinlock, like the mutex and the lock in Redis, let <em>one</em> in at a time. But <code>estoque</code> has a limit that is not a single slot: PostgreSQL accepts a fixed number of connections, and each connection serves one query at a time. On Black Friday, 200 orders arrive at the same time on each replica. The thing that controls how many get in, and what to do with the rest, is the semaphore.</p>

<OneLiner>A counting semaphore is a lock with <strong>N slots</strong>: whoever arrives when all are taken waits for one to be released. The <strong>connection pool</strong> of <code>estoque</code> is a 10-slot semaphore in front of PostgreSQL.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>The semaphore created by Dijkstra (1965) has a count and two operations: <strong>P</strong> (take a slot, which the paper calls <em>Wait</em>) and <strong>V</strong> (give it back, the <em>Signal</em>). With the count at 0, whoever calls P waits until someone calls V.</li>
  <li><code>estoque</code> uses a pool of <strong>10 connections</strong> to PostgreSQL. Each order takes a connection, runs the query and returns it. The 11th order waits for a slot. In Go, the pool is a buffered channel: <code>make(chan struct&#123;&#125;, 10)</code>. Sending takes a slot, receiving releases it.</li>
  <li>A <strong>mutex</strong> is a 1-slot semaphore <strong>with an owner</strong>: whoever locked is whoever unlocks. The binary semaphore, with 1 slot, has no owner, and that is the difference between the two, not the count.</li>
  <li>The pool size math comes from Little's law (1961): connections needed = throughput × time per query. With 500 orders per second and 20 ms per order, 500/s × 0.020 s = <strong>10 connections</strong>.</li>
  <li>On Black Friday, <strong>200</strong> simultaneous orders arrive per replica. With a pool of 10 and 20 ms each, the throughput stays at <strong>500/s</strong>. The first 10 finish in 20 ms, and the last one, which is in the 20th batch, finishes in 20 × 20 ms = <strong>400 ms</strong>. The average is 20 ms × (1 + 2 + … + 20) ÷ 20 = <strong>210 ms</strong>. Without the pool, 200 connections would go past PostgreSQL's <code>max_connections</code> (<strong>100</strong> by default), and the database would answer <code>FATAL: sorry, too many clients already</code>.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: scaling too much takes the database down</h3>

<p>Black Friday heats up and autoscaling takes <code>estoque</code> up to <strong>12 replicas</strong>. Each one has its own pool of 10, and the math works out on each replica: 12 × 10 = <strong>120 connections</strong>, more than the 100 the database accepts. From the 101st on, PostgreSQL refuses, and orders fail, even though every replica is healthy. <strong>Adding more replicas does not help</strong>: the bottleneck moved to the database.</p>

<p>There are three remedies. The first is a <em>pooler</em> in front of the database (for example PgBouncer), which merges the connections of all replicas into a few real connections. The second is reducing the pool per replica: 12 × 8 = 96. The third is raising <code>max_connections</code>, at a memory cost on the database server. This limit comes back in module 14 (saturation) and in module 25 (bulkhead).</p>

<h3 class="sec">A semaphore is not a worker pool</h3>

<p>The lab (<code>semaphore/main.go</code>) creates <strong>one goroutine per task</strong> and only limits <strong>how many run at the same time</strong>: capacity 3, 10 foods, 2 s each. The tasks go out in ⌈10 ÷ 3⌉ = 4 waves of 2 s, or <strong>8 s</strong>, and the article's log matches that. A <strong>worker pool</strong> is something else: a fixed number of workers that keep reading tasks from a queue (a channel). The similar effect hides differences in cost and queue behavior.</p>

<p>Other languages use the second design. Tomcat has by default a pool of 200 threads to serve requests (<a href="https://tomcat.apache.org/tomcat-10.1-doc/config/http.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener"><code>maxThreads</code> attribute</a>), and Node.js has a pool of 4 threads (libuv) for files, DNS and cryptography (<a href="https://docs.libuv.org/en/v1.x/threadpool.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">libuv documentation</a>).</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>The limit belongs to the resource, not to the code.</strong> The database accepts 100 connections, and all replicas share that number.</li>
  <li><strong>A pool is a queue with slots.</strong> Whoever goes past 10 waits, and the last one's wait (400 ms) is the queue math.</li>
  <li><strong>Little sizes it.</strong> Throughput × query time tells how many connections the load asks for.</li>
  <li><strong>Scaling replicas has a ceiling.</strong> Replicas × pool has to fit in <code>max_connections</code>.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <h4 class="deep-h">A bit more slack in the database math</h4>
  <p>PostgreSQL reserves a few connections for superusers (<code>superuser_reserved_connections</code>, 3 by default), so with <code>max_connections</code> = 100 applications have 97 at hand. The 12 × 8 = 96 fit by a small margin, and 12 × 10 = 120 blows past it.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Correction to the source article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>A semaphore is not a worker pool.</strong> The article treats the two as the same thing. The semaphore limits how many executions happen at the same time (the lab creates one goroutine per task); the worker pool has a fixed number of workers that consume from a queue. Also: the article says the binary semaphore is "similar to the Mutex", and the difference is in ownership, since the mutex has an owner and the binary semaphore does not.</li>
  </ul>
