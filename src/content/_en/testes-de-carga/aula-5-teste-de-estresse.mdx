<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Staircase.</strong> <code>ramping-arrival-rate</code> with levels: 100, 200, 250 and 300 orders/s, back to 100. Each climb lasts 20 s and each level <strong>45 s</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>10 s windows.</strong> k6's CSV lets you compute the p95 of each window, not only the cumulative one.</li>
  <li><strong>Indicators.</strong> CPU, memory, network, I/O, <strong>pool</strong> and <strong>queue</strong>, as the article says, along with p95, errors and <em>goodput</em>.</li>
  <li><strong>Target level.</strong> 200/s is what the plan promises; 250 and 300 are the adverse.</li>
  <li><strong>Descent.</strong> The return to 100/s measures whether the store <strong>recovers</strong> and how long it takes.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">The staircase on plan 2</h3>

<p>The environment was "plan 2": 10 pods on <code>pedidos</code>, 15 on <code>pagamentos</code> and the 2× dedicated shard for <code>mkt-01</code>. There were 59,567 requests.</p>

<p>Total: average 419 ms, p95 1.48 s, p99 1.92 s, failures <strong>0.54%</strong>, <code>dropped_iterations</code> 182, exit code <code>99</code>. The target (p95 ≤ 800 ms) <strong>holds up to 250/s</strong>: the worst window was 738 ms. At 300/s it falls, and errors appear. The <em>goodput</em> at 300/s sits at 280–293/s, <strong>below what arrives</strong>.</p>

<h3 class="sec">The recovery</h3>

<p>When the load goes back to 100/s, the first window still has a p95 of 1,247 ms, and the second is already at 244 ms. The store comes back in <strong>one to two windows</strong> (10 to 20 s). Recovery is fast because the bottleneck <strong>discards what already expired</strong>: after 2 s the simulator throws the request away, and the queue empties on its own. The same effect shows up in the spike, in the next lesson.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: more load, fewer orders</h3>

<p>In the "today" environment the capacity is ~40 orders/s. When the load goes past that, the <em>goodput</em> <strong>drops</strong> from ~40 to ~26/s. <code>pagamentos</code> spends capacity on requests whose customer <strong>already gave up</strong> (at 2 s) and less is left for those still waiting. The result goes against intuition: <strong>more load means fewer completed orders</strong>.</p>

<p>This is the kind of finding only stress gives. The article says stress "exceeds the normal flow"; what the store gains is knowing that, above the ceiling, pushing more doesn't help.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Stress shows the order of degradation.</strong> First the p95 (250 to 300/s), then the errors (300/s), last the goodput.</li>
  <li><strong>Recovery is part of the result.</strong> In 10 to 20 s the p95 goes back to ~220 ms, because the bottleneck discards what expired.</li>
  <li><strong>Above the ceiling, goodput drops.</strong> From ~40 to ~26/s on "today": the store does less when it receives more.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={3}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Correction to the base article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>"Stress is a much higher load".</strong> What stress measures is <strong>how the system degrades and recovers</strong>. The article doesn't mention the drop in <em>goodput</em> above the ceiling, which here went from 40 to ~26/s.</li>
    <li><strong>The lab's report doesn't add up.</strong> "Went past the limits at around 1,500 users" and "2,000 for 1 hour with an increase of 500 every 15 min" are incompatible: 4 steps of 500 go from 500 to 2,000 in 1 h, and the load does <strong>not</strong> stay at 2,000 for 1 h.</li>
  </ul>
