<p class="ponte">In the last lesson the test got clear targets. The full test, though, goes through four services and three shards, and when it breaks it doesn't say which piece broke. Before it, the article says to measure <strong>one unit alone</strong>.</p>

<OneLiner>The <strong>pre-test</strong> measures <strong>one replica</strong> first, then N, <strong>until the link changes</strong>: the point where computing power stops being the bottleneck and the next dependency takes over.</OneLiner>

<DoIt>
  <p>In the figure, in the <strong>Pre-test</strong> scenario, press <strong>Add a pod</strong> until <code>pedidos</code> has 3 and watch the <strong>link</strong> change: the capacity goes up by 30 per pod until <code>pagamentos</code> becomes the limit.</p>
</DoIt>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>One replica.</strong> Ramp until the target breaks. One <code>pedidos</code> pod has 30/s nominal (33 ms) and ~21/s sustainable at 70%.</li>
  <li><strong>One more.</strong> Double and repeat: 60/s nominal with 2 pods, 90/s with 3.</li>
  <li><strong>Until the link changes.</strong> When the capacity stops growing linearly, the bottleneck is another piece.</li>
  <li><strong>Isolate the dependencies.</strong> With <code>pagamentos</code> "infinite" (100 pods), only <code>pedidos</code> limits.</li>
  <li><strong>Validate the async part.</strong> Turn on <strong>one</strong> consumer replica with a full queue and see if it holds up.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">The <code>pedidos</code> pre-test</h3>

<p>The environment was <code>pedidos</code> with 1, 2 and 3 pods, <code>pagamentos</code> with 100 pods (the "infinite") and plenty of shards. The ramp was 2 min up to 38 × N orders/s, with the rate offered:</p>

<p>Capacity scales <strong>linearly, ~30 per pod</strong>, while <code>pedidos</code> is the link, and the target falls <strong>close to the nominal</strong>. The ramp measures the ceiling with a delay (lesson 7), which is why the target point comes a little above the nominal on pods 1 and 2.</p>

<h3 class="sec">The link changes</h3>

<p>Now the proof. The real "today" has 3 pods on <code>pedidos</code>, and the table above would say <strong>~88/s</strong>. The "today" breakpoint (lesson 7) broke at <strong>36/s</strong>. The difference is <code>pagamentos</code>, which has 2 pods of 20/s and is the link. With <code>pagamentos</code> "infinite", three pods would go past 85/s; with 2 pods, the chain stops at ~40/s nominal.</p>

<h3 class="sec">The async pre-test</h3>

<p>For queue consumers the article changes the procedure: you <strong>dam up</strong> a huge amount of messages, <strong>turn on a single replica</strong> and check that it consumes only the planned throughput <strong>without dying</strong>. The store's math comes from module 23: each <code>entregas</code> partition processes 40 events/s. A replica with a queue full of millions of events can't pull more than it can process, or memory blows up and the replica dies before the second one turns on.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: dependencies with no limit</h3>

<p>Testing <code>pedidos</code> with <code>pagamentos</code> "infinite" says 3 pods go past 80/s. It is an honest answer to a question nobody asked: <code>pagamentos</code> is not infinite. The real "today" breaks at <strong>36/s</strong>. The pre-test only counts if the dependencies have the <strong>same shape</strong> as in production, or if the report says they don't.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>One replica gives the plan's math.</strong> ~30/s per <code>pedidos</code> pod, linear until the link changes.</li>
  <li><strong>The link changes.</strong> 3 pods say 88/s; with a 2-pod <code>pagamentos</code>, "today" breaks at 36/s.</li>
  <li><strong>An unlimited dependency lies.</strong> The result only holds for the configuration it was measured in.</li>
</WhyItMatters>
