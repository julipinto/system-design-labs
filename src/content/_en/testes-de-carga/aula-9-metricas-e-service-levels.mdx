<p class="ponte">In the last lesson the test got a question, a mix and a rate. The last piece is missing: <strong>how do you know it passed?</strong> A k6 table has about twenty numbers, and the article answers that "passing" means meeting the <strong>service levels</strong> that the product research defined.</p>

<OneLiner>Passing is <strong>measuring against a target</strong>: the <strong>p95</strong> (not the average), the <strong>error rate</strong>, the <strong>saturation</strong> and the <strong>measuring point</strong>. The targets become k6 <em>thresholds</em> and, later, SLOs.</OneLiner>

<h3 class="sec">Average, p95 and p99 are not the same number</h3>

<p>In the 30 orders/s test the <strong>average</strong> was 157.5 ms: green, far from any target. The <strong>p99</strong> was 498.6 ms, three times higher, and it is what 1 in every 100 customers felt. The p95 of 381 ms sits in the middle. Percentiles also <strong>don't add up</strong> (module 14): the chain's p95 is not the sum of the services' p95s, but the percentile of the complete responses.</p>

<h3 class="sec">Per window vs. cumulative</h3>

<p>k6 computes the p95 of the whole test from the start. A 3 min ramp with 100 s of calm pulls the cumulative down: lesson 7's <code>abortOnFail</code> stopped at <strong>128 s</strong> with a cumulative p95 of <strong>894 ms</strong>, but the p95 <strong>per window</strong> had already gone past 800 ms in the <strong>100–110 s</strong> window (820 ms). The cumulative serves for the final summary; the window serves to locate the break.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What "passed" means</h3>

<p>Two targets, and both have to pass: p95 ≤ 800 ms <strong>and</strong> errors ≤ 0.1%. The widget below reads the numbers and says whether it passed, and, given a table of windows, where lesson 7's three points are.</p>

<Widget name="leitor" title="Result reader" hint="Pick a real result or type avg, p95, p99, errors and rate: the widget says whether it passed against p95 ≤ 800 ms and errors ≤ 0.1%. Pick a table of windows to find the target, errors and collapse points." />

<p>The author's report has a case the reader fails. In the <em>spike</em>, the expectation is "average response time &lt; 400 ms" and the result, "400 ms". Four hundred <strong>is not</strong> less than four hundred. The text says "came close to the limit", but by the report's own rule the result is <strong>failed</strong>, and the target needs an explicit sign: <code>&lt;</code> or <code>≤</code>.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: the green average and the late abort</h3>

<p>Reading the 157 ms <strong>average</strong> and declaring the plan approved forgets the 499 ms p99 and the <code>504</code>s that appear from ~42 orders/s on "today" (6.8% in lesson 7).</p>

<p>The second mistake is trusting <code>abortOnFail</code> as the marker of the break: it fires <strong>late</strong> because it looks at the cumulative. For a cut that counts, the right way is to evaluate by window in the CSV or in a dashboard like Prometheus (histograms) and use the abort only as protection so the system doesn't go down.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>p95 and p99 show what the average hides.</strong> A 157 ms average had a 499 ms p99.</li>
  <li><strong>The measuring point is part of the target.</strong> k6, <code>lb-loja</code> and the service measure different times for the same order.</li>
  <li><strong>The window locates; the cumulative summarizes.</strong> The abort at 128 s came ~20 s after the window that crossed 800 ms.</li>
</WhyItMatters>
