<p class="ponte">At the end of module 14, every bottleneck we solved moved somewhere else. The catalog page (CPU at 99% on one replica, p99 of 2,000 ms) was fixed by cloning, from 10 to 14 replicas. <code>pagamentos</code> (40 orders/s) was fixed the same way, from 2 to 4. After both, the ceiling became the <code>pedidos</code> database, at <strong>150 orders/s</strong>, and cloning a service does not move it. We need a map to decide where to grow, and the map fits in three questions.</p>

<OneLiner>The Scale Cube is a <strong>vocabulary of three questions</strong>: do I copy the service (X axis)? Do I split what is different (Y axis)? Do I split what is the same but too big (Z axis)? The store already answered two of them without knowing the names.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>The model has an owner and a date. <strong>AKF Partners</strong> created the cube in <strong>2007</strong>, first published it on their blog and later in the books by Abbott and Fisher (<em>The Art of Scalability</em>, 2nd edition from 2015, and <em>Scalability Rules</em>). They call it a common language for talking about scale.</li>
  <li>The <strong>X axis</strong> is cloning. It means N <strong>identical</strong> copies of the service behind a load balancer, and each copy takes <strong>1/N</strong> of the load (Richardson's math). It is the easiest axis and the one that scales transactions.</li>
  <li>The <strong>Y axis</strong> is splitting by function. You divide <strong>what is different</strong>: by <strong>verb</strong> (a use case, like <code>checkout</code>) or by <strong>noun</strong> (an entity, like <code>clientes</code>), each service with its own data. This is the decomposition into microservices.</li>
  <li>The <strong>Z axis</strong> is splitting by data. You divide <strong>what is the same but too big</strong>: all copies run the same code, each one serves only a subset of the data (by <code>cliente_id</code>, by region, by SKU, by customer type), and a <strong>router</strong> looks at the attribute and picks the destination.</li>
  <li>With three axes, the store gets <strong>coordinates</strong>: (X replicas, Y functions, Z partitions). At the end of module 5 it was an 8 vCPU monolith with one database, <strong>(1, 1, 1)</strong>. Module 6 split the program into 7 services, modules 8 and 9 added the gateway and the BFFs (with <code>bff-app</code> and <code>bff-web</code>, 7 + 2 = <strong>9 deployments</strong>, 30 pods in module 10), and module 7 cloned the catalog to 10. Today the store is at <strong>(10, 9, 1)</strong>. Z is the axis it has not used yet.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: the right axis for the wrong symptom</h3>

<p>The search takes 900 ms per request, and the obvious reaction is to clone: from 10 to 20 replicas. The queue shrinks, because there are more servers. But <strong>each search still takes 900 ms</strong>, because the time of one request comes from its code, and X does not touch the code. The cost is real: if the same move were made on the catalog, going from 10 to 20 replicas of 1 vCPU doubles the CPU bill from 10 to 20 vCPU. Scale does not fix slow code. The way out for the search is an index (module 4) or a cache (module 5).</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>The cube is the map, not the territory.</strong> It names three ways to grow and leaves the decision to the symptom.</li>
  <li><strong>The store already moved on two axes.</strong> Y in module 6 and X in module 7, without the names. Z is what is missing.</li>
  <li><strong>Symptom before axis.</strong> The diagnosis table starts from the measurement, and the 900 ms search shows that sometimes the answer is no axis.</li>
  <li><strong>Shared vocabulary.</strong> Saying "the problem is Z" shortens an architecture meeting.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the source article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>"Microservices modeling".</strong> The article presents the cube as microservices modeling. For AKF and for Richardson, the cube is a model to scale and segment services <strong>and data</strong>, and only the Y axis is microservices. X and Z also apply to a monolith, and AKF applies it to "products", not only to services.</li>
    <li><strong>"Width, height and depth".</strong> These names do not appear in the primary sources. AKF says X = duplication and cloning, Y = functional decomposition and segmentation, Z = partition by customer or data boundary, and Richardson uses the same. This module says "clone, split by function, split by data".</li>
    <li><strong>References.</strong> The Medium link ("Achieving Scalability with Scale Cube") is not used. What is used: AKF (the article "The Scale Cube" and the 2007 posts on splitting applications and databases), Richardson (microservices.io) and Wikipedia.</li>
  </ul>

<p class="gancho">The cube is left with three questions, and the cheapest one to answer is the first: copy. The store did that with the catalog and the math closed. What is left is knowing <strong>where X, the easiest axis, stops</strong>: <code>pedidos</code> gained replicas and stayed stuck at 150 orders/s.</p>
