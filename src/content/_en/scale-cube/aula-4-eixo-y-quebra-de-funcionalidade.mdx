<p class="ponte">In the last lesson ten copies of the catalog were enough, but they all did the same thing. Ten copies of the whole monolith would still be one program, with one database and three teams waiting on each other. Splitting what is <strong>different</strong> is Y's job, and the store already did that in module 6.</p>

<OneLiner>Y splits what is different so that <strong>each part scales its own way and has an owner</strong>: by verb (a use case, like <code>checkout</code>) or by noun (an entity, like <code>clientes</code>), each service with its own data.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>There are two ways to cut, and Richardson gives them names. By <strong>verb</strong>: a use case, <code>checkout</code>, which crosses <code>carrinho → pedidos → pagamentos → estoque → entregas</code>. By <strong>noun</strong>: an entity, <code>catalogo</code>. Caio's order 1053, at 8:05 PM on Black Friday (1 blue mug at 19.90 plus Rio shipping of 12.00, <strong>31.90</strong>; the mug stock goes from 38 to 37), crosses <strong>five services</strong>, with four hops of 5 ms: <strong>+20 ms</strong> (module 6).</li>
  <li>Each piece has a different <strong>profile</strong>, and that is what Y lets you handle separately. The article talks about CPU versus I/O. The catalog is CPU and cache. <code>estoque</code> waits on the database (20 ms of waiting for 2 of CPU, module 11). <code>relatorios</code> is a 4 vCPU batch. In a monolith the three share the same machine size.</li>
  <li>The math shows up in vCPU. Ten copies of the 8 vCPU monolith cost <strong>80 vCPU</strong>. The 7 services on Black Friday add up to <strong>25 vCPU</strong>: catalog 10 × 1, cart 3 × 1, orders 3 × 1, payments 2 × 1, stock 2 × 1, deliveries 1 × 1 and reports 1 × 4. That is 55 vCPU less, <strong>69%</strong>.</li>
  <li>Y is the <strong>only axis that increases organizational scalability</strong>. The 3 teams (Storefront on the catalog, Checkout on cart, orders and payments, Logistics on stock and deliveries) stop fighting over a 40-minute deploy (module 8) and ship on their own, each one owning its code and its data.</li>
  <li>Each service has <strong>its own</strong> database. That is what gives failure isolation and availability: <code>relatorios</code> can lock up its own database without touching the checkout one. A Y without its own data is a distributed monolith.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: a service per table</h3>

<p>Someone splits <code>itens_pedido</code> into its own service, because "it is another entity". The "my orders" screen shows 20 orders. Before, it was a <code>JOIN</code> of about <strong>2 ms</strong>; now it is 1 call to list the orders and <strong>20 calls</strong> to fetch the items of each one: 21 × 5 ms = <strong>105 ms</strong> of network alone.</p>

<p>And there is a second, less visible loss: the <strong>single transaction</strong> is gone. The refund in module 6 was a <code>ROLLBACK</code>; with two services and two databases, it becomes the saga from module 19. Y cuts along the business boundary, and <code>itens_pedido</code> has no boundary of its own: it is part of the order.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Y splits what is different.</strong> Verb (<code>checkout</code>) or noun (<code>catalogo</code>), each with its own profile, team and database.</li>
  <li><strong>The math is measured.</strong> 25 vCPU against 80, 69% less, with the same 10 for the catalog.</li>
  <li><strong>It is the organization's axis.</strong> Independent deploys instead of 40 minutes of waiting.</li>
  <li><strong>It cuts along the business boundary.</strong> A service per table trades a 2 ms <code>JOIN</code> for 105 ms of network.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<p class="gancho">Y and X do not split the <strong>data</strong>: <code>pedidos</code> is a service of its own, and even cloned into 7 replicas it still talks to a single database, which stops at 150 orders/s. What is left is how to split what is the <strong>same but too big</strong>. That is the Z axis.</p>
