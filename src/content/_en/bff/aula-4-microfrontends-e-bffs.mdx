<p class="ponte">In lesson 3 each channel got its own BFF. But the website's screen is still a single one, and three teams touch it: the same three from module 6, Vitrine, Checkout and Logística. If the <code>bff-web</code> belongs to everyone, every deploy becomes a queue.</p>

<OneLiner>In a microfrontend, each team owns a piece of the screen and the BFF that feeds that piece, and publishes both without waiting for the others.</OneLiner>

<p>Conway's Law, from module 6, says the system ends up with the shape of the organization that builds it. Here that becomes a drawing: three teams, three screen pieces, three BFFs.</p>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>The website page is a shell that loads three pieces, one from each team.</li>
  <li>Each piece asks only its own BFF for data: the storefront asks <code>bff-vitrine</code>, the cart asks <code>bff-checkout</code>, tracking asks <code>bff-rastreio</code>.</li>
  <li>Each BFF composes the calls to the services its piece uses, as in lesson 2. The <code>pedidos</code> shows up in two, and that's fine: the data belongs to the service, the shape belongs to the BFF.</li>
  <li>When Logística changes tracking, it publishes the piece and the <code>bff-rastreio</code> together. Vitrine and Checkout don't even find out.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<p>On the page, the shell is small. This is an example written for the lesson, in the style of Cam Jackson's article on micro frontends, with one HTML element per team:</p>

<pre><code>{String.raw`<body>
  <loja-vitrine   bff="/web/vitrine"></loja-vitrine>    <!-- Vitrine team script -->
  <loja-checkout  bff="/web/checkout"></loja-checkout>  <!-- Checkout team script -->
  <loja-rastreio  bff="/web/rastreio"></loja-rastreio>  <!-- Logística team script -->
</body>`}</code></pre>

<p>What if it goes wrong? Each team has its own BFF, and the temptation is for each BFF to solve the business rule its own way.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Independent deploy.</strong> Each team publishes its screen piece and its BFF when it's ready.</li>
  <li><strong>A clear owner.</strong> When the tracking screen breaks, the team that answers the alert is the one that wrote it.</li>
  <li><strong>The risk:</strong> three BFFs calculating shipping in three ways. Lena sees one value on the storefront and another at checkout.</li>
  <li><strong>The rule:</strong> the business rule lives in the service that owns it. The BFF only <strong>composes and adapts</strong>.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={5}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">How to tell if a rule belongs to the BFF</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>If it changes the <strong>format</strong> (renaming a field, cutting a photo, sorting the list, formatting the price), it belongs to the BFF.</li>
    <li>If it changes the <strong>result</strong> (how much shipping costs, who gets a discount, whether the order can be canceled), it belongs to the service.</li>
    <li>If two BFFs need the same calculation, it belongs to the service and both call it.</li>
  </ul>
  <h4 class="deep-h">What microfrontends are</h4>
  <p>Cam Jackson, in the article published by Martin Fowler, describes the idea as splitting a monolithic frontend into smaller parts, each owned by a team, integrated on the page by build, iframe, JavaScript or Web Components. The article also lists the costs: more load for the browser and more operational complexity.</p>
