<p>In the last lesson each key-value pair lived alone. But the store wants to say on the plush cat's page: "whoever bought this also bought that". That depends on links between Lena, Bia, the cat and the mugs, and links are what the graph model works with.</p>

<OneLiner>In the graph model, entities are nodes, relationships are edges, and both are first-class data; so following a node to its neighbor is an operation of the database, not a JOIN assembled in the query.</OneLiner>

</DoIt>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>Each customer and each product becomes a <strong>node</strong>, with properties: the Lena node has <code>cidade: Recife</code> and the gato de pelúcia node has <code>preço: 89.90</code>.</li>
  <li>Each purchase becomes an <strong>edge</strong> with a direction and a name: <code>(Lena)-[COMPROU]→(gato de pelúcia)</code> is order 1042 and <code>(Bia)-[COMPROU]→(gato de pelúcia)</code> is 1043. The blue mug (caneca azul) also gets edges from Lena (order 1044) and Bia (order 1046).</li>
  <li>The question "whoever bought the cat also bought what?" is a <strong>traversal</strong>: from the cat, follow COMPROU backwards to Lena and Bia. From each of them, follow COMPROU forwards to the other products.</li>
  <li>The result: both bought the <strong>blue mug</strong>. That is the recommendation for whoever is looking at the plush cat.</li>
  <li>In SQL, the same question needs joining the <code>pedidos</code> table with itself. With millions of orders and many hops, each join costs more than the previous one. In the graph, each hop starts from the node you are on and looks only at its neighbors.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li>When the answer lies in the links (friends of friends, products bought together, the path between two points), the graph stores the link ready-made instead of recomputing it.</li>
  <li>Real uses: product recommendation, social networks, fraud detection (accounts linked to the same card), threat analysis and logistics.</li>
  <li>For summing a column or looking up by id, the graph is not the tool. It wins where the question looks like a map.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={3}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Questions the graph answers well</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>"Students in the morning class who live in the same neighborhood and have an average above 8."</li>
    <li>"Friends of friends who live in the same city and worked at the same company."</li>
    <li>"Whoever bought the plush cat also bought what?" (the store's).</li>
  </ul>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Performance care</h4>
  <p>The cost depends on the <strong>degree</strong> of the nodes (how many edges leave each one). A product that millions of people bought is a heavy node, and passing through it on every traversal is expensive. Good queries start from an uncommon node and limit the depth.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Graph is NoSQL, and it is about relationships</h4>
  <p>The source article says NoSQL databases "have no direct relationships". That describes key-value and document. The graph database is the exception: it was built for relationships. The model does give up the table format, though, which is why it belongs to the NoSQL family.</p>
