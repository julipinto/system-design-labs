<p class="ponte">Last lesson the <code>pedidos</code> topic had 3 brokers, 3 partitions and 3 consumer groups, all on paper. The author's public repository has a lab that brings up a real Kafka with a producer and two consumers in Go. It proves half of lesson 5 (the key, the groups, the lag) and leaves the other half out, and it is worth knowing which is which before running it.</p>

<OneLiner>The lab brings up <strong>one Kafka broker in KRaft</strong>, a producer that publishes one event per millisecond and two consumers from different groups: you can see the key, each group's offsets and the lag growing, but <strong>not the replication</strong>.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>Inside <code>kafka/</code>, a <code>docker compose up -d</code> brings up <code>confluentinc/cp-kafka:7.5.0</code> in <strong>KRaft</strong> (no ZooKeeper), with <strong>1 node</strong> acting as both broker and controller, on ports 9092 and 9093, and <code>kafka-ui</code> on port 8080. Retention is 7 days: <code>KAFKA_LOG_RETENTION_HOURS: 168</code>.</li>
  <li>The <strong>producer</strong> (<code>producer/main.go</code>) uses IBM's <strong>sarama</strong> client with <code>RequiredAcks = WaitForAll</code>, <code>Retry.Max = 5</code> and Snappy compression. The key is <code>pedido.ID</code> (<code>PEDIDO-00001</code>, <code>PEDIDO-00002</code>…), the topic is <code>pedidos</code>, and a 1 ms <code>ticker</code> fires one event per tick. <code>SendMessage</code> is synchronous, so the real throughput is limited by the time of each send. The log shows <code>Partition: N | Offset: M</code>.</li>
  <li>Two <strong>consumers</strong> read the same topic in different groups. <code>consumer_estoque</code> is in the <code>estoque-consumer-group</code> group, and <code>consumer_faturamento</code> is in <code>faturamento-consumer-group</code>, with a <code>time.Sleep(100ms)</code> per event. Both start at <code>OffsetOldest</code>, commit the offset by <strong>auto-commit every 1 s</strong> and call <code>MarkMessage</code> only <em>after</em> processing.</li>
  <li>The <code>partition_key/</code> folder shows the real key. <code>setup.sh</code> creates the <code>pedidos-particionados</code> topic with <strong>3 partitions</strong> and replication factor 1. The producer uses <code>NewHashPartitioner</code> with the key equal to <code>cliente_id</code> and a <code>SequenciaCliente</code> field, to prove that each customer's sequence comes out in order within its partition.</li>
  <li>The mapping to the store is direct. The lab's <code>pedido.ID</code> is the store's <code>pedido_id</code>, and the lab's <code>faturamento</code> is the <code>entregas</code> group. What <code>partition_key</code> teaches is that <strong>the choice of key decides what stays in order</strong>: with <code>cliente_id</code>, one customer's events stay in order; with <code>pedido_id</code>, one order's events, which is what the store wants.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">Five things the lab does not do like the lesson</h3>

<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>The <code>pedidos</code> topic has 1 partition.</strong> It is created on its own (<code>KAFKA_AUTO_CREATE_TOPICS_ENABLE</code>) and the <code>num.partitions</code> default is 1. To see partitions, use <code>pedidos-particionados</code>.</li>
  <li><strong>The cluster has 1 broker and replication factor 1.</strong> Replication is <strong>not demonstrated</strong>: the leader, the followers and the ISR from lesson 5 only exist in the story.</li>
  <li><strong>Three slips in the code itself.</strong> <code>setup.sh</code> calls <code>docker exec kafka ...</code>, but the compose container is named <code>kafka-kraft</code>: when run, both commands (create and describe the topic) fail with <code>No such container: kafka</code> and the script still prints "Setup completo!". It also tells you to run <code>go run consumer.go</code>, which does not exist in the folder. And the producer's comment says "50 mensagens" (50 messages), but the loop only stops at 15,000.</li>
  <li><strong>Go's partitioner is not Java's.</strong> sarama's <code>NewHashPartitioner</code> uses <strong>FNV-1a</strong>; the Java client uses <strong>murmur2</strong>. I checked the package (v1.42.1, the producer's, and v1.46.3, the <code>partition_key</code>'s) and there is <strong>no</strong> <code>NewMurmur2Partitioner</code>: the compiler answers <code>undefined: sarama.NewMurmur2Partitioner</code>. To match Java, you have to pass your own murmur2 hasher to <code>NewCustomPartitioner</code>. <code>NewReferenceHashPartitioner</code>, which has the name of something that imitates Java, also uses FNV-1a.</li>
  <li><strong>The producer and the slow consumer run at different paces.</strong> The producer tries 1 event per millisecond; <code>faturamento</code> processes 10 per second. The lag grows, and that is what <code>kafka-ui</code> shows.</li>
</ol>

<p>The fifth point deserves numbers. With 3 partitions, the same <code>pedido_id</code> lands in different places depending on who produces: <strong>1051</strong> goes to <strong>p2</strong> in sarama and to <strong>p0</strong> in Java; <strong>1049</strong> goes to p2 and p0; <strong>1050</strong> goes to p0 and p2. Whoever mixes clients in different languages on the same topic breaks the order of an order with no error at all. I ran both sides and the numbers in the paragraph held up. On a 3-partition topic, Kafka's console producer (Java) sent 1049 to p0, 1050 to p2 and 1051 to p0; sarama's <code>NewHashPartitioner</code> computed p2, p0 and p2.</p>

<pre><code>{String.raw`key     Java (murmur2)   sarama NewHashPartitioner (FNV-1a)
1048    p2               p1
1049    p0               p2
1050    p2               p0
1051    p0               p2
1052    p2               p1`}</code></pre>

<p>Of the five keys, none landed on the same partition in both clients.</p>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: the consumer goes down between processing and committing</h3>

<p><code>faturamento</code> processes an event and <strong>goes down before the next auto-commit</strong>, which goes out every 1 s. When it comes back, it rereads from the last committed offset: up to <strong>1 s of events</strong>. At 100 ms per event, that is <strong>about 10 duplicated events</strong>. This is the at-least-once of lesson 5 showing its face. I tested with <code>docker kill</code> (SIGKILL) on the <code>faturamento</code> consumer: the last event processed was offset 135, and the offset committed in Kafka was 130. When it came back, it restarted at 130, and <strong>6 events</strong> (130 to 135) were processed twice. The 1 s window is the ceiling of about 10; the number for each crash depends on when it falls relative to the commit. The consumer only started receiving events again 8 s after the crash, until the group reassigned the partition.</p>

<p>There are two fixes. The first is <strong>idempotence</strong> by <code>evento_id</code>: the consumer keeps the ids already seen (the repeated <code>e-502</code> from module 6) and ignores the repeat. The second is a <strong>manual commit</strong> after writing the result. And the lab's other flaw, the producer much faster than the consumer, is lesson 5's lag live: the LAG column in <code>kafka-ui</code> only grows.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>The lab proves the key and the groups.</strong> Each group has its own offset and its own lag.</li>
  <li><strong>It does not prove replication.</strong> 1 broker and factor 1: <code>acks=all</code> here waits for nobody besides the leader.</li>
  <li><strong>Mixing clients breaks the order.</strong> FNV-1a and murmur2 send 1051 to different partitions.</li>
  <li><strong>Auto-commit has a window.</strong> A crash in the middle of it repeats about 10 events.</li>
</WhyItMatters>
