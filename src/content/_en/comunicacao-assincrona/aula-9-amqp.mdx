<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>The arithmetic shows the problem. In a single queue, Lena's email waits <strong>50,000 ÷ 100 = 500 s</strong>, or <strong>8 min 20 s</strong>. With <strong>separate queues</strong>, one for transactional and another for promotional, it goes out in <strong>under 1 s</strong>. We need a way for the message to reach the right queue without the producer knowing which one it is.</li>
  <li>That is what <strong>AMQP 0-9-1</strong>, RabbitMQ's protocol, does. A TCP connection (port <strong>5672</strong>, opened with the 8-byte header <code>41 4D 51 50 00 00 09 01</code>, "AMQP" and the version) carries several virtual <strong>channels</strong>. The producer publishes to an <strong>exchange</strong> with a <strong>routing key</strong>; the exchange routes through the <strong>bindings</strong> to the <strong>queues</strong>; the consumer reads from the queue and sends the <strong>ack</strong>. The difference from Kafka is this: the producer <strong>does not choose the queue</strong>, the broker routes.</li>
  <li>Routing depends on the <strong>exchange type</strong>. <strong>Direct</strong> matches the whole key (it suits commands like <code>cobrar</code>). <strong>Topic</strong> matches patterns, with <code>*</code> for one word and <code>#</code> for zero or more. <strong>Fanout</strong> copies to every bound queue. <strong>Headers</strong> matches on headers. And there is the <strong>default exchange</strong>, with no name, which routes by a key equal to the queue name.</li>
  <li>The store uses a <strong>topic exchange</strong> called <code>emails</code>. The keys are <code>email.pedido.confirmacao</code>, <code>email.pedido.enviado</code> and <code>email.promo.bf</code>. The queues: <code>emails.transacionais</code> (binding <code>email.pedido.*</code>), <code>emails.promocionais</code> (<code>email.promo.#</code>) and <code>emails.auditoria</code> (<code>email.#</code>, which gets a copy of everything). The confirmation of 1051 lands in <code>transacionais</code> and in <code>auditoria</code>; the Black Friday promotion lands in <code>promocionais</code> and in <code>auditoria</code>.</li>
  <li>The <strong>guarantees</strong> come from pieces that are switched on one by one. <strong>Manual ack</strong> makes the message go back if the consumer goes down without confirming. <strong><code>prefetch</code></strong> limits how many unacknowledged messages each consumer receives. A <strong>durable</strong> queue plus a <strong>persistent</strong> message (<code>delivery_mode = 2</code>) survive a restart. <strong>Publisher confirms</strong> tell the producer the broker stored it. The <strong>DLX</strong> (<em>dead letter exchange</em>) receives the message that was rejected without requeueing, expired (TTL) or overflowed the queue limit, and sends it to <code>emails.dlx</code> and <code>emails.dlq</code>. <strong>Quorum queues</strong>, with Raft, replicate across nodes.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<h3 class="sec">What if it goes wrong: the routing key with a typo</h3>

<p>In a deploy, the producer starts publishing <code>emails.pedido.confirmacao</code> (<strong>plural</strong>, a typo) instead of <code>email.pedido.confirmacao</code>. No binding matches that key: neither <code>email.pedido.*</code> on the transactional queue nor <code>email.#</code> on the audit queue. The exchange <strong>finds no queue</strong> and <strong>silently drops the message</strong>. The producer gets success and thinks the email was sent. Lena never receives the confirmation of 1051, and no error shows up anywhere.</p>

<p>There are three remedies that add up. The first is <code>mandatory = true</code>: the broker <strong>returns</strong> the unroutable message to the producer. The second is an <strong>alternate exchange</strong>, which receives whatever nobody routed, and an alert when it receives something. The third is testing the keys and the bindings together, at deploy time.</p>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Separate queues take the transactional email out from behind.</strong> 8 min 20 s becomes under 1 s for Lena's email.</li>
  <li><strong>The broker routes.</strong> The producer only knows the exchange and the key; new queues attach by binding.</li>
  <li><strong>The guarantee is built in layers.</strong> Ack, prefetch, persistence, confirms and DLX: each one closes a failure.</li>
  <li><strong>With no route, silence is the default.</strong> <code>mandatory</code> and the alternate exchange bring the error back.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

  <h4 class="deep-h">AMQP 0-9-1 and AMQP 1.0</h4>
  <p>The article describes the 0-9-1 model and calls it just "AMQP". <strong>AMQP 1.0</strong> (OASIS, ISO/IEC 19464) is a <strong>different protocol</strong>, with no exchanges. RabbitMQ 4.0 supports it natively; in 3.x, through a plugin.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Corrections to the source article</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Protocol version.</strong> The article mixes the two: what it describes (exchange, binding, queue) is <strong>0-9-1</strong>.</li>
    <li><strong>Channels.</strong> "Each session is an independent connection" is wrong: <strong>channels</strong> are virtual and share <strong>one</strong> TCP connection, which is why they are cheap.</li>
    <li><strong>Routing key and binding key.</strong> The article mixes them up. The <strong>routing key</strong> is the one the producer sends; the <strong>binding key</strong> is the pattern of the link between the queue and the exchange.</li>
    <li><strong>What is missing.</strong> The <strong>default exchange</strong>, <strong>publisher confirms</strong>, <strong>prefetch</strong>, the <strong>DLX</strong> and <strong>quorum queues</strong>. And, in the article's examples, the queue is durable but the message is not persistent.</li>
  </ul>
