<p>RAID 5's rebuild leaves the store with no margin: while it runs, pedidos.db depends on all the other disks working. A second parity removes that risk. It costs one more disk of space and more expensive writes.</p>

<OneLiner>RAID 6 is a RAID 5 with two parities, so it withstands the loss of two disks at the same time, in exchange for one more disk of space and slower writes.</OneLiner>

<DoIt>
  <p>In the figure, click <strong>Next step</strong> and let D2 burn out. See A2 and A5 rebuilt from the first parity and notice Q, which stays in reserve. At step 6, D4 burns out too: see P and Q rebuild the two missing blocks.</p>
</DoIt>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>With <strong>5 disks of 1 TB</strong>, each stripe has 3 data blocks and <strong>2 parity blocks</strong>. Both rotate among the disks.</li>
  <li>The first parity (<strong>P</strong>) is the XOR, same as RAID 5. The second (<strong>Q</strong>) is calculated a different way (Reed-Solomon math), so the two are different.</li>
  <li>With one failure, RAID 6 does the same as RAID 5: P1 xor A1 xor A3 = 0110, which is A2. And Q stays in reserve.</li>
  <li>With two failures, P alone isn't enough. With two equations (P and Q), you can find <strong>two unknown blocks</strong>.</li>
  <li>Capacity: (5 - 2) x 1 TB = <strong>3 TB</strong> usable (60%). It tolerates <strong>2 failures</strong>. Each small write becomes <strong>6 operations</strong> (write penalty 6).</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li>It fixes RAID 5's rebuild risk: if a second disk fails (or a URE shows up) during the rebuild, there is still margin.</li>
  <li>Recommended for large volumes and data that stays for years, with big disks and many of them.</li>
  <li>It costs slower writes and one more disk of overhead. For the same 3 TB usable, RAID 6 needs 5 disks and RAID 5 needs 4.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={3}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">The math</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Minimum:</strong> 4 disks (with 4 disks of 1 TB, 2 TB usable are left).</li>
    <li><strong>Usable capacity:</strong> (N - 2) x size. 5 x 1 TB: 3 TB (60%).</li>
    <li><strong>Failures tolerated:</strong> 2.</li>
    <li><strong>Write penalty:</strong> 6 (read data, old P and Q, write data, new P and Q).</li>
  </ul>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Quick comparison with RAID 5 (1 TB disks)</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>RAID 5 with 4 disks: 3 TB, 1 failure, write penalty 4.</li>
    <li>RAID 6 with 5 disks: 3 TB, 2 failures, write penalty 6.</li>
    <li>Random writes with 150 IOPS per disk: 4 x 150 / 4 = 150 (RAID 5) against 5 x 150 / 6 = 125 (RAID 6).</li>
  </ul>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Number of disks</h4>
  <p>The more disks in the group, the higher the chance of failures during the rebuild. That is why RAID 6 is the common choice for large groups.</p>
