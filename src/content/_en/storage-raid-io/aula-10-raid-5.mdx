<p>RAID 1's mirror saved pedidos.db, but it used half the disks. With 4 disks you can spend just one: instead of copying each block, the controller keeps a calculation that lets it rebuild the lost block. That calculation is the <strong>parity</strong>.</p>

<OneLiner>RAID 5 spreads the data and a parity across the disks, so it survives the loss of any one of them while spending only one disk of space.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li>The store uses <strong>4 disks of 1 TB</strong>. In each stripe, 3 disks hold data and 1 holds the <strong>parity</strong> (P). The parity <strong>rotates</strong>: P1 sits on D4, P2 on D3.</li>
  <li>The parity is the <strong>XOR</strong> of the stripe's blocks. With A1 = 1011, A2 = 0110 and A3 = 0101, the parity is P1 = 1011 xor 0110 xor 0101 = <strong>1000</strong>.</li>
  <li>D2 dies and takes A2 and A5. To rebuild A2, the controller XORs what is left in the stripe: A1 xor A3 xor P1 = 1011 xor 0101 xor 1000 = <strong>0110</strong>. A2 is back. A5 comes out the same way.</li>
  <li>Until D2 is replaced, the file is complete, just slower (degraded mode). During the <strong>rebuild</strong>, the controller reads all the other disks and recalculates each block of D2.</li>
  <li>Capacity: (4 - 1) x 1 TB = <strong>3 TB</strong> usable (75%). It tolerates <strong>1 failure</strong>.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li>A good ratio of space to protection: you lose only 1 disk, however many there are. The more disks, the smaller the fraction lost.</li>
  <li><strong>Writes are expensive</strong>: each small write becomes 4 operations (write penalty 4).</li>
  <li>The <strong>rebuild</strong> reads all the other disks. With large disks, it takes long and is risky: if another disk fails in that window, the file is lost.</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={5}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">The math</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>Minimum:</strong> 3 disks.</li>
    <li><strong>Usable capacity:</strong> (N - 1) x size. 4 x 1 TB: 3 TB (75%).</li>
    <li><strong>Failures tolerated:</strong> 1.</li>
    <li><strong>Write penalty:</strong> 4.</li>
  </ul>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Why a small write costs 4 I/Os</h4>
  <ol class="flow steps">
    <li>Read the old block.</li>
    <li>Read the old parity.</li>
    <li>Calculate the new one: new P = old P xor old data xor new data.</li>
    <li>Write the new block and the new parity (2 writes).</li>
  </ol>
  <p>Total: 2 reads + 2 writes = 4 operations. With 4 disks of 150 IOPS, random writes drop from 600 to 150 IOPS.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Rebuild and URE: what if the disks were 10 TB? (example)</h4>
  <ul>
    <li>Rebuilding a 10 TB disk at 150 MB/s: 10,000,000 MB / 150 = 66,667 s, more than 18 hours. In practice, days with the store live. With the figure's 1 TB disks, it is about 2 hours.</li>
    <li>In a RAID 5 of 4 disks of 10 TB, the rebuild reads the other 3 disks in full: 30 TB = 2.4 x 10^14 bits.</li>
    <li>Common disks have 1 <strong>URE</strong> (an unrecoverable read error) per 10^14 bits. By that math, you expect about 2 to 3 errors during the rebuild. With no spare parity, each error is a loss. With 1 TB, the math drops to ~0.24 expected errors.</li>
  </ul>
  <h4 class="deep-h">Hot spare</h4>
  <p>A spare disk, powered on and empty: when a disk fails, it steps in by itself and the rebuild starts right away, without waiting for someone to swap the disk.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">See it on Linux</h4>
  <pre><code>{"sudo mdadm --create /dev/md0 --level=5 --raid-devices=4 /dev/sd[b-e]\ncat /proc/mdstat        # shows \"recovery = 12.3%\" during the rebuild"}</code></pre>
