<p>In the earlier lessons the balancer chose the replica by looking at load or IP. But the store doesn't have only <code>catalogo</code>: it also has <code>carrinho</code> and <code>pedidos</code> (module 6), and the same address <code>loja.com.br</code> has to take each request to the right service. For that, the balancer needs to read the request. And what it can read depends on the <strong>layer</strong> it works at, the same layers as the OSI model in module 1.</p>

<OneLiner>A layer 4 balancer sees only the outside of the envelope (IP and port) and forwards the whole connection, fast; a layer 7 one terminates TLS, opens the HTTP request and can route by path, at the cost of more CPU and more latency.</OneLiner>

<HowItWorks>
<ol class="flow steps">
  <li><strong>Layer 4 (transport).</strong> The balancer sees only <code>TCP 177.38.20.5:51514 → 203.0.113.10:443</code>: the IP and port of whoever asks, the IP and port of whoever receives. It doesn't read HTTP. It picks a replica and <strong>forwards the whole connection</strong> to it, without opening the content, which stays encrypted.</li>
  <li><strong>Layer 7 (application).</strong> The balancer opens the request and reads <code>GET /produtos/42 HTTP/1.1</code>, <code>Host: loja.com.br</code> and <code>Cookie: sessao=…</code>. With that, it can decide by path, by host, by a header or by the cookie.</li>
  <li><strong>The price of opening.</strong> To read the HTTP, the balancer has to <strong>terminate TLS</strong>: the <code>loja.com.br</code> certificate lives on it (module 1, lesson 5), and it talks plain HTTP, or a new encrypted connection, to the replica.</li>
  <li><strong>What that buys.</strong> L7 routes by path: <code>/produtos/*</code> goes to <code>catalogo</code>, <code>/carrinho/*</code> to <code>carrinho</code> and <code>/pedidos/*</code> to <code>pedidos</code>. With L4, the three services would each need an address and a port.</li>
  <li><strong>The cost.</strong> In the store's example, L7 spends more CPU and adds about <strong>1 ms</strong> per request, and L4 adds almost nothing, about <strong>0.1 ms</strong>. Both numbers are examples, but the proportion is the idea: reading costs.</li>
</ol>
</HowItWorks>

<WhyItMatters>
  <li><strong>Speed or intelligence.</strong> L4 fits where throughput is what matters (millions of connections, protocols that aren't HTTP). L7 fits where routing depends on the content.</li>
  <li><strong>One address for several services.</strong> L7 lets the store have a single <code>loja.com.br</code>, with module 6's services hidden behind it.</li>
  <li><strong>The same step serves other jobs.</strong> Whoever terminates TLS and reads the request can also cache, compress and reject bad requests. This is where the balancer starts to look like a reverse proxy (lesson 2).</li>
</WhyItMatters>

<Deep min={5}>
  <h4 class="deep-h">The two layers in the cloud</h4>
  <ul>
    <li><strong>AWS:</strong> the <em>Application Load Balancer</em> (ALB) is L7 (HTTP, HTTPS, gRPC), with routing by path, host and header. The <em>Network Load Balancer</em> (NLB) is L4 (TCP, UDP, TLS), built for throughput and low latency.</li>
    <li><strong>Mixed:</strong> it's common to have an L4 in front, splitting the traffic among several L7s, which in turn route to the services.</li>
  </ul>
  <h4 class="deep-h">What L4 does with the connection</h4>
  <p>Without reading the application, L4 only needs all the packets of one connection (the same source and destination IP and port pair) to reach the same replica. That's why it can use IP hash or consistent hashing (lesson 7) easily: the decision comes from the numbers on the envelope.</p>
  <h4 class="deep-h">A note on TLS</h4>
  <p>Terminating TLS at the balancer is the article's TLS <em>offload</em>: it takes the work of encrypting and decrypting off the replicas. In exchange, the traffic between the balancer and the replica goes in the clear, unless it's encrypted again (which many people do).</p>
